# South African alcohol brands: togetherness on social, and where brands crossed the line

Research date: 16 October 2026. Scope: South Africa, 2015 to October 2026, weighted to 2020–2026. Copy lines are quoted verbatim where a source carried them. "(search excerpt)" means the line came from a search-result summary of the page and the page itself was not opened. Treat those as less certain.

Context for the writer: the governing text is the ARB's **Appendix A: Alcohol Industry Communication Code of Conduct** (© Advertising Regulatory Board, 25 March 2024). It is administered by the Drinks Federation of South Africa (DF-SA) and enforced by the ARB. Its clauses are quoted under the "getting it wrong" question below, because every ruling here turns on them.

## SAB / AB InBev SA brands: how they do togetherness, and their responsible-drinking programmes

### Takeaway
SAB's strongest togetherness work hangs on shared national rituals (Heritage Day braai, Springbok friendship, women's gatherings) and on causes (Carling Black Label's anti-GBV #NoExcuse). Its responsibility layer is the "SAB Sharp" platform, plus Castle Free 0.0% positioned as a "pacer". The same company has also been ruled against twice since 2025: the Brutal Fruit beach ad and a Castle Lager influencer post.

### Inferences
- SAB's togetherness template is: *a national ritual or cause, a real community or real friends, and the beer as a participant rather than the cause of the good time.* Castle's Heritage Day braai is about the record and the gathering, not the beer delivering relief or reward.
- Carling's #NoExcuse is the clearest SA example of a brand naming alcohol's harms in its own social content and drawing praise for it. Its line "getting drunk is not an excuse" shows that responsibility content can be brand-building, not just a legal footer.
- The Jana Swart post shows the acceptable register: national pride ("nothing can stop us South Africans"), a participatory prompt ("your best Braai Hacks"), and vernacular ("Ons gaan nou braai!"), with #ad and the 18+ icon.
- The 2010 "Groot man of laaitie" billboard and its withdrawal show that quantity-as-status copy draws complaints even from big brands.

### Gaps
- Castle Lite ("Extra Cold", "Unlock") and the National Braai Day partnerships were not checked in this pass. I can't confirm whether Castle Lite 0.0 exists in SA.
- I found no SAB campaign called "#RethinkTheDrink". The DGMT op-ed links to rethinkyourdrink.co.za, which appears to be a public-health resource, not an SAB property (unverified).
- No primary SAB/AB InBev SA document stating Smart Drinking goal progress was retrieved.

## Distell / Heineken Beverages SA brands: Savanna, Hunter's, Bernini, Strongbow and others

### Takeaway
Heineken Beverages (ex-Distell) builds on humour (Savanna), "easy refreshment" and chill (Hunter's), and women's celebration (Bernini). It was named Brand of the Year at the 2026 Loeries. It also owns the clearest SA ruling on the "drink as relief" line: the ARB ordered a Strongbow Gold Apple TV ad withdrawn in June 2025 for presenting cider as the answer to workplace stress.

### Cited Findings
**Savanna**

**Bernini**

**Strongbow (ARB ruled against, June 2025)**
- The ARB ruled against a TV commercial for Strongbow Gold Apple cider after a consumer complaint that it "promoted alcohol as a way to manage workplace stress". The ad opens on "a stressed office worker, overwhelmed by a large pile of paperwork, while being berated by a colleague". Screens then show "WARNING" and "NEVER STOP PLAYING". The worker's desk is cleared, a paper aeroplane "transforms into a bottle of Strongbow Gold Apple cider", he "leans back in his chair, feet propped up on the desk", and "all visible computer monitor screens display the time as 17:00". It closes on "Never stop playing" and an under-18 disclaimer — [News24, 5 Jun 2025](https://www.news24.com/business/consumer-lookout/strongbow-ad-promotes-booze-to-help-manage-work-stress-watchdog-finds-20250605-0715)
- The defence was that the aeroplane-to-bottle moment was "a lighthearted, metaphorical representation of 'leaving work behind'". The ARB found viewers would "reasonably" read it "as portraying alcohol consumption as a solution to workplace stress", which "directly ties emotional relief and relaxation to the product" (clause 1.4.1). Outcome: the ad was ordered withdrawn and may not be used again in its current form — [News24, 5 Jun 2025](https://www.news24.com/business/consumer-lookout/strongbow-ad-promotes-booze-to-help-manage-work-stress-watchdog-finds-20250605-0715)
- News24 paraphrases clause 1.4.1 as prohibiting "any suggestion… that alcohol consumption can reduce stress, improve relaxation, or solve personal or professional challenges". The Code's actual 1.4.1 text says "solve personal problems, overcome inhibitions…". Stress and sedation are covered more explicitly by 1.6.4 ("sedative, or tranquiliser"). See the Code clauses below.

**Heineken Beverages at the Loeries 2026**
- Named Brand of the Year at the 2026 Loeries for work across Heineken, Windhoek, JC Le Roux and Bernini, among others. Heineken's "Bar de Change" (LePub Johannesburg) won the Live Events Grand Prix — [Bizcommunity](https://www.bizcommunity.com/article/heineken-beverages-named-brand-of-the-year-at-the-2026-loeries-636181a)

### Inferences
- **The Strongbow ruling is the closest SA precedent to "get your Fuego fix".** Every element the ARB objected to, a stressed state followed by the drink followed by relief, plus the 17:00 clock, is implied by "fix" in two syllables. "Fix" also suggests craving or dependence. A light-hearted or metaphorical defence failed in Strongbow's case.
- Savanna shows that dry humour is a durable, accepted register. Its humour sits on the brand's character (the monkey, the boss jokes), not on the drink fixing the situation. Note, though, that "Boss Jokes" set in workplace/bonus season is close to Strongbow territory; humour about the boss is fine, the drink as the escape from the boss is not.
- Savanna's response to #SavannaChallenge is a useful template for a brand being tagged in risky UGC: state publicly that the brand didn't start or endorse it, restate pacing and limits, and invite people to tag responsible moments instead.
- Bernini's "rewards those who are audacious in romance, and their careers" sits close to 1.4.1 (no implied social, professional or personal success). No complaint was found, but it is the kind of line to avoid.
- Hunter's "Chillism" is a chill/refreshment frame. It reads as acceptable when chill means an easy social vibe, and risky if it shifts to the drink producing calm (1.6.4 sedative/tranquiliser).

### Gaps
- Savanna's "It's dry but you can drink it" tagline, and Klipdrift ("Met eish"/Klippies and Coke), Amarula ("Name Them, Save Them"), Esprit, Smirnoff Storm SA and Hansa, were not verified in this session.
- The Strongbow advertiser's corporate name isn't stated in the News24 article (presumably Heineken Beverages, unverified). The full ARB ruling text was not retrieved.
- Details of the Hunter's "Chillism" creative (copy, social posts) were not retrieved.

### Inferences
- Patch is on Checkers shelves with a Margarita and a Paloma, the same flavours and retailer as Doña Fuego. "Share of cooler box" competition therefore includes a local agave/cocktail RTD, not just Brutal Fruit, Bernini and Savanna.
- Wildebeest shows the ARB gives humour and nostalgia some latitude, but a "more = happier" line still drew a formal caution. RTD copy about volume (stocking up, "fill the cooler box", "another round") should stay on the occasion, not on quantity bringing happiness.
- The two 2026 seltzer/hard-soda rulings show RTDs being scrutinised on "light"/calorie messaging. "Guilt-free", "light" or calorie lines would be high-risk for Doña Fuego (see Code 1.6.3 below).

## SA examples of getting it wrong: rulings, complaints and backlash

### Takeaway
The 2024 Code explicitly endorses the togetherness frame. It says ads showing "responsible drinking among adults as a relaxed, sociable, and enjoyable part of a well-lived life" have a role to play (1.2.1). It bans the "fix" frame: no solving problems (1.4.1), no curative, stimulant, sedative or tranquiliser claims (1.6.4), and no drug culture (1.7.2). Recent SA cases map almost one-to-one onto a social manager's risk list: stress relief (Strongbow 2025, withdrawn), beach drinking (Brutal Fruit 2025, withdrawn or amend), undisclosed influencer posts (Castle Lager 2026), "more = happier" (Wildebeest 2026, cautioned), low-calorie claims (two RTDs, 2026), and consumer drinking challenges (Savanna 2021).

### Cited Findings
**The Code, quoted verbatim from ARB Appendix A (25 March 2024)** — [ARB Appendix A: Alcohol Industry Communication Code of Conduct](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/lappendix-a_alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct-mar-2024.pdf)
- 1.2.1: "Commercial Communications that depict responsible drinking among adults as a relaxed, sociable, and enjoyable part of a well-lived life has a role to play in reinforcing a responsible approach to drinking."
- 1.2.2: "should never show drinkers as losing control or having diminished movement, speech, or vision due to drinking alcohol."
- 1.2.3: "must not show or imply drinking before or during activities requiring a high degree of skill, concentration, or sobriety including driving, operating machinery, sporting, or other athletic activities."
- 1.2.5: "should make it clear that drinking is a personal choice and never disparage people who choose to abstain from drinking or to consume alcohol responsibly."
- 1.4.1: "should not imply that drinking resulted in business, social, professional, academic, athletic, financial, or other personal success, nor that drinking can solve personal problems, overcome inhibitions, shyness or social barriers, or enable social acceptance."
- 1.4.2: no suggestion of "sexual indulgence or permissiveness… nudity or near-nudity… gender stereotypes; or claim or imply that drinking can contribute directly to sexual success or seduction."
- 1.6.3: no health claims, "nor may they include terms like 'healthier', 'better for you', or 'safer'."
- 1.6.4: "Commercial Communications may not claim that alcohol has curative qualities, or offer alcohol as a performance enhancer, stimulant, sedative, or tranquiliser."
- 1.7.2: may not "portray or in any way endorse or glamourise illicit drugs, drug culture, organised crime, or local gang activity."
- 1.9.1: brand names and packaging must not use "colloquialisms for alcohol (e.g., 'dop,' 'booze,' or 'grog')" or convey "the intoxicating effect of alcohol".
- 1.5.2: "Non-label forms of Commercial Communications should not feature or depict a product's alcohol content unless the alcohol content by volume is at or below 2.5% (i.e., is a lower alcohol product)."
- 1.1.4: celebrities and social media personalities "paid or unpaid" must be, and appear to be, "at least 21 years of age… behaving in an acceptable and responsible manner… in an age-appropriate setting."
- 1.1.5: "All alcohol brand-controlled websites and social media pages must have an age verification mechanism."
- 1.1.7: on brand-controlled pages with user-generated content, "all people who are clearly visible must be, and appear to a reasonable person to be, over the legal drinking age unless they are obviously incidental and in the background."
- 1.8.1.1: the authorised message is "Don't Drink and Drive / Not for Persons Under 18 / Pregnant Women Should Not Drink Alcohol, Be Responsible"; "Where a small screen format is used, for example, Instagram or Twitter, the 'Not for Under 18' token may be used as an alternative."
- 1.10.2–1.10.4: alcohol-free and non-alcohol drinks "may only be promoted and sold to people of legal drinking age". Their communications must comply with the whole Code. The carve-out allowing them to be promoted for driving or business-lunch occasions, or "as a sociable option for avoiding over-consuming alcohol", applies only where "the beverages do not carry an alcohol brand name".
- Age-rule versions conflict: the 2020 Aware.org code (successor to the ARA code) said people depicted drinking may not be or appear younger than 25, with a controlled exception down to 21. The 2024 ARB Appendix A text retrieved here specifies 21 for celebrities and influencers — [Bizcommunity on the 2020 code](https://www.bizcommunity.com/Article/196/12/200336.html); [ARB Appendix A](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/lappendix-a_alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct-mar-2024.pdf)

**Rulings and cases (most recent first)**
- **Castle Lager / SAB influencer post (ARB, July 2026; partly upheld).** Cape Town influencer Jana Swart posted Castle Lager-branded World Cup content (before SA v Mexico) without #ad or #sponsored. SAB argued it was organic content on her personal X account (@JanaSwart1), separate from her paid "brand advocate" Instagram (@MzansiSoccerGirl). The ARB called this distinction "confusing at best and disingenuous at worst". It ruled: "Given that the brand assets were prominently used, and that the product being promoted is an alcohol-related product, the directorate concludes that the promotion should not have been published without a relevant disclaimer stating that the influencer is #sponsored or that it is an #ad, even if it is not the sponsored account." The age-warning complaint failed because the "18+ SAB SHARP responsibility icon" was shown. Sanction: remove the post or add the disclaimer immediately — [News24, 28 Jul 2026](https://www.news24.com/business/companies/ad-regulator-blasts-sab-in-landmark-influencer-ruling-20260728-1113); [EWN](https://www.ewn.co.za/regulator-cracks-down-on-ambiguous-influencer-posts-with-ruling-against-sab/)
- **Wildebeest Brandewyn (ARB, 11 May 2026; dismissed with caution):** "Brandy is like love – the more you get, the happier you are." See the RTD section above — [Bizcommunity, 21 May 2026](https://www.bizcommunity.com/article/arb-clears-wildebeest-brandewyn-ad-accused-of-promoting-binge-drinking-067655a)
- **Solstice Seltzer (17 Apr 2026) and South Side Hard Soda (Jul 2026): unsubstantiated low-calorie claims, upheld** — [Bizcommunity](https://www.bizcommunity.com/article/this-alcohol-brands-low-calorie-claim-didnt-hold-up-rules-arb-392298a); [News24](https://www.news24.com/business/companies/sa-spirit-cooler-slammed-for-misleading-low-calorie-claim-20260717-0970)
- **Strongbow Gold Apple (ARB, June 2025; upheld, withdrawn):** workplace stress, then cider, then feet on the desk at 17:00, with "NEVER STOP PLAYING". See the Heineken Beverages section above — [News24, 5 Jun 2025](https://www.news24.com/business/consumer-lookout/strongbow-ad-promotes-booze-to-help-manage-work-stress-watchdog-finds-20250605-0715)
- **Brutal Fruit beach ad (ARB, early February 2025; upheld, withdraw or amend).** The ad aired on DStv Channel 121. It "features a bottle of Brutal Fruit on a colourful beach towel" followed by "a series of scenes of friends enjoying Brutal Fruit". The complainant said beach drinking is illegal, that "drunken individuals often drown on South African beaches", that broken glass injures children, and that "it is not necessary to drink alcohol to enjoy South African beaches" — [IOL, 6 Feb 2025](https://iol.co.za/news/south-africa/brutal-fruit-advert-condemned-for-promoting-illegal-beach-drinking-4a483eae-c8cc-4177-a207-f8c4521e5798)
- In that case SAB said the bottle was closed and no one drank on the beach. The ARB called the closed-bottle argument "disingenuous", because a consumer "will not scrutinise each frame, but will most likely believe that all the settings are places the drink can be consumed". It ruled: "There is no doubt in the minds of the Directorate that watched as a whole, the commercial condones and in fact encourages bringing alcohol to the beach and consuming it on the beach. This is illegal." The clause cited was 3.3 (no condoning illegal activity) — [Bizcommunity, 7 Feb 2025](https://www.bizcommunity.com/article/arb-ruling-should-alcohol-brands-advertise-on-the-beach-365388a)
- A critic notes that both 2025 upheld ads (Strongbow and Brutal Fruit) "were pulled from TV but remain online" — [DGMT op-ed, 26 Sep 2025](https://dgmt.co.za/op-ed-what-the-alcohol-and-advertising-industries-get-away-with/)
- **#SavannaChallenge (Jan–Feb 2021):** consumer-generated drinking challenge videos trended around the end of the alcohol bans; the brand disowned it publicly. Separately, a liquor board "slam[med] [a] viral video of youth celebrating return of booze sales" on 4 February 2021 (headline only seen) — [TimesLIVE, 6 Jan 2021](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/south-africa/2021-01-06-savanna-distances-itself-from-irresponsible-and-damaging-viral-trend/); [TimesLIVE, 12 Feb 2021](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/south-africa/2021-02-12-whats-in-this-savanna-savannachallenge-resurfaces-after-booze-ban/)
- **Carling Black Label "Groot man of laaitie? Vra vir die volle 750ml" (2010):** an excessive-drinking complaint by Sonke Gender Justice; SAB withdrew the ad before an ASA ruling — [DGMT op-ed](https://dgmt.co.za/op-ed-what-the-alcohol-and-advertising-industries-get-away-with/)
- ARB jurisdiction: in 2022 the Supreme Court of Appeal overturned a High Court finding that the ARB had no jurisdiction over non-members. The ARB can still rule on non-member ads "for the guidance of its members" (search excerpt) — [Webber Wentzel](https://webberwentzel.com/News/Pages/sca-decision-on-advertising-regulatory-boards-powers-a-victory-for-brand-owners-and-consumers.aspx)

### Inferences
- **"Get your Fuego fix" triggers several clauses at once.** "Fix" is drug slang for a dose (1.7.2 drug culture). It implies craving or dependence and a remedy (1.6.4 "curative qualities… sedative, or tranquiliser"; 1.4.1 "solve personal problems"). Strongbow shows that a "lighthearted, metaphorical" defence of an implied relief arc fails. Sixty60 on-demand delivery strengthens the craving-to-relief reading.
- The same logic covers SA social staples such as "Monday survival kit", "you've earned it", "after the week we've had", "load-shedding coping", "wine o'clock"-style time stamps and the 17:00 clock. None was found ruled on in SA social specifically, but each fits the Strongbow pattern.
- **Beach content is a specific Cape Town summer risk.** Under the Brutal Fruit precedent, a closed can on a beach towel, or a montage that moves from beach to drinking scenes, is enough. Keep cans off sand and towels and away from the water's edge, even unopened. Beach and sunset imagery without product, or product at a home braai or picnic on private property, is safer.
- **ABV in posts:** read literally, 1.5.2 means Doña Fuego's "7%" should not be a feature of social posts or ads (beyond the label visible on the can). Lines like "7% of pure fire" are risky. Worth confirming with the code researcher or counsel.
- **Non-alc Margarita:** because it carries the Doña Fuego name, the 1.10.4 carve-out ("for when you're driving", "the sober-option margarita", "pace yourself") appears unavailable, and the product must be marketed only to adults (1.10.2). SAB's Castle Free "pacer" messaging sits in tension with this literal reading, so get a view from counsel.
- **Influencers and staff:** the Castle ruling means every branded post needs #ad or #sponsored on every account a paid person uses, including "personal" ones. The "Not for Under 18" token is acceptable on Instagram stories.
- **Humour helps but does not immunise.** Wildebeest survived on comic, nostalgic framing, Strongbow didn't. The difference: Strongbow's narrative made the drink the solution, while Wildebeest's line was judged unlikely to be taken literally. A cheeky Fuego voice is fine; a cheeky voice in which the can rescues someone is not.
- Nothing found suggests the industry codes are lightly enforced on social. Complaints come from individual consumers, including an addiction counsellor and a beach-goer, so any post can become the next test case.

### Gaps
- The ARB rulings database (arb.org.za) wasn't searched directly. Rulings come via press coverage, so other alcohol social-media rulings from 2020–2026 may be missed.
- I found no SA ruling or press report on alcohol posts implying COVID-lockdown coping, "Monday"/"weekend survival", sexualised content, people appearing under 25, or drink-driving on social, 2020–2026. The under-25 search returned only UK ASA cases.
- The exact wording of the ARB Code "clause 3.3" cited in the Brutal Fruit ruling wasn't retrieved. It appears to be from the earlier "Code of Commercial Communications for Alcoholic Beverages" numbering.

## Commentary after the 2020–21 sales bans, and the industry's "responsible" repositioning

### Takeaway
During and after the five bans, the industry's public voice was economic (jobs, taxes) and compliance-led: BASA's "we can't afford another ban", outlet cut-offs, SAB Sharp, and the 0.0% push. Public-health voices used the period to argue that SA has "a collective drinking problem" and to push advertising restrictions. That pressure peaked again in 2025, when the EFF called for a total ad ban and a former alcohol-ad strategist wrote a widely republished op-ed.

### Cited Findings
- The ARB's chief executive said during the 2020 lockdown that "there is a ban on the sale of alcohol, but there is not a ban on advertising", and that brands kept advertising so consumers would remember them when sales resumed (search excerpt) — [IOL, 6 May 2020](https://iol.co.za/news/politics/2020-05-06-lockdown-in-sa-booze-ban-does-not-prohibit-advertising-alcohol/)
- In a study of early-COVID alcohol policy framing, SAB tweeted an image of a press release arguing against a "suspension of alcohol and beer sales". Critics said the industry used social media to present misleading information on the restrictions (search excerpt) — [PMC: Alcohol policy framing in SA during early COVID-19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10537160/)
- BASA urged responsible, moderate drinking at the Level 2 reopening (Aug 2020). In September 2020 it said it would identify outlets breaching Level 2 rules and "cut off their supply", blacklist those whose licences were revoked, and back a hotline run by the Consumer Goods Council — [ECR](https://www.ecr.co.za/news/news/beer-association-calls-moderate-responsible-drinking/); [Business Day, 2 Sep 2020](https://www.businessday.co.za/bd/national/2020-09-02-beer-brewers-to-cut-off-lockdown-offenders-to-avoid-another-ban/)
- BASA's Black Friday 2020 appeal: "we can't afford another ban". It said the bans had cost about 7,400 jobs, R14.2 billion in lost sales and more than R7.4 billion in lost taxes and excise — [TimesLIVE, 25 Nov 2020](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/south-africa/2020-11-25-we-cant-afford-another-ban-beer-body-wants-you-to-slow-down-on-the-booze-this-black-friday/); [Polity, 24 Nov 2020](https://www.polity.org.za/article/black-friday-basa-calls-for-responsible-trade-and-consumption-of-alcohol-2020-11-24)
- SAB cancelled R2.5 billion of planned investment after the second ban (search excerpt) — [ESM Magazine](https://www.esmmagazine.com/drinks/south-african-breweries-cancels-165m-investment-latest-alcohol-ban-119180)
- A UCT study found unnatural deaths fell more during full sales bans than during curfews. Daily Maverick headlined it "'Hello, my name is South Africa... we have a collective drinking problem'". The lead author called for a national conversation that includes alcohol marketing restrictions — [Daily Maverick, 5 Jul 2021](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2021-07-05-hello-my-name-is-south-africa-we-have-a-collective-drinking-problem-alcohol-bans-had-more-impact-than-curfews-study-finds/)
- A Bhekisisa investigation (2020) traced the never-enacted 2013 Control of Marketing of Alcoholic Beverages Bill, which would have banned alcohol advertising and sponsorship, and industry-funded opposition to it — [Bhekisisa, 2 Jun 2020](https://bhekisisa.org/health-news-south-africa/2020-06-02-government-said-no-to-alcohol-sales-during-lockdown-but-it-didnt-say-no-to-the-industrys-money/)
- Daily Maverick (2023) argued that alcohol brands spend much of their ad space on mandatory warnings, and that non-alcoholic brands are moving into territory beer and spirits brands once held, forcing a rethink of how they market — [Daily Maverick, 29 May 2023](https://www.dailymaverick.co.za/article/2023-05-29-navigating-opportunities-for-alcohol-brands-in-an-economic-slowdown/)
- Corné Kritzinger, who worked on alcohol brands in advertising from 2002 to 2013, wrote an op-ed republished by DGMT (26 Sep 2025), Media Marketing (29 Oct 2025) and The Media Online (Nov 2025). Verbatim lines: "Our job was to sell, not solve." "Beer equals masculinity; wine equals class; cider means fun." "The ad may not say 'this will make you successful', but in the ad, successful people drink, creating the life others aspire to have." He argues the 2016 Liquor Amendment Bill (ad ban except at point of sale) "has stalled", and that alcohol companies "embedded themselves" in Nedlac's process from 2017 to 2021 — [DGMT](https://dgmt.co.za/op-ed-what-the-alcohol-and-advertising-industries-get-away-with/); [Media Marketing](https://www.media-marketing.com/en/opinion/why-the-proposed-ban-on-alcohol-advertising-has-a-valid-point/); [The Media Online](https://themediaonline.co.za/2025/11/what-the-alcohol-advertising-industry-and-alcohol-producers-get-away-with/)
- Julius Malema (EFF) called for a total ban on alcohol advertising in August 2025, saying it glamorises drinking while hiding its social harms. Traders' bodies called the push "self-sabotage" — [IOL, 25 Aug 2025](https://iol.co.za/news/politics/2025-08-25-malema-calls-for-total-ban-on-alcohol-ads-says-industry-hides-drinkings-social-harm/); [The Citizen](https://www.citizen.co.za/news/south-africa/ban-alcohol-advertising-self-sabotage-says-traders-council/)
- In 2026 Bhekisisa questioned whether the industry-funded ARB should be able to block public-health messaging, after it ruled against a sugar health ad — [Bhekisisa, 19 Feb 2026](https://bhekisisa.org/health-news-south-africa/2026-02-19-our-advertising-regulator-is-funded-by-the-food-and-beverage-industry-should-it-be-allowed-to-block-public-health-messaging/)

### Inferences
- Alcohol advertising in SA is politically live: there is a stalled ad-ban bill, a 2025 EFF ban call and recurring op-eds. A small brand's careless story can be screenshotted into that debate. A "fix" line is exactly the kind of evidence critics cite.
- The industry's post-ban move was to make responsibility visible: SAB Sharp, BASA's moderation appeals and the 0.0% push. Brands that show visible pacing, choice and non-alc options align with where the large players went.

### Gaps
- I found no evidence of a campaign called "Buy Local Beer". No 2021 Liquor Traders' campaign was located.
- MarkLives and Fin24 commentary specifically on post-ban alcohol marketing wasn't found in this pass.
- I found no SA brand social posts during the bans that were criticised for implying coping or escape. The documented lockdown backlash was consumer-generated (#SavannaChallenge, a viral youth video), not brand-authored.

## SA vernacular around shared occasions, and how brands use it respectfully

### Takeaway
Braai, Heritage Day (24 September), shisa nyama, December holidays and the beach are the shared-occasion language SA alcohol brands lean on. The respectful pattern is to centre the people, food and ritual, with inclusive and participatory framing ("No DNA, just RSA!", "your best Braai Hacks", "Ons gaan nou braai!"). The documented pitfalls: brands commercialising heritage, reinforcing a gendered "men at the fire with beer" image, and placing product on beaches, where drinking is illegal.

### Cited Findings
- Jan Scannell ("Jan Braai") launched National Braai Day in 2005. Archbishop Emeritus Desmond Tutu became patron and it was renamed "Braai4Heritage" to gain National Heritage Council approval. The movement's site notes the tradition is also called "Chisa Nyama, Braai and Ukosa" (search excerpt) — [Braai.com via Wayback Machine](https://webcf.waybackmachine.org/web/20210324013843/https://braai.com/about)
- A 2014 News24 column ("National Braai Day a day of forgetting") argued that supermarket chains, abattoirs and breweries used the national day to boost consumerism, and that "the corporate advertising reinforced the idea that braaing is gendered, with men at the fire drinking beer" (search excerpt) — [News24, 17 Sep 2014](https://www.news24.com/news24/columnists/tomolefe/National-Braai-Day-a-day-of-forgetting-20140917); [OkayAfrica: 8 perspectives on the Braai Day debate](https://www.okayafrica.com/8-perspectives-on-south-africas-braai-day-debate/180653)
- Castle Lager's 2025 Heritage Day "World's Biggest Braai" used a Soweto street billboard grill, free boerewors rolls and a national record ("No DNA, just RSA!") (search excerpt) — [SA Rugby Mag](https://www.sarugbymag.co.za/castle-lager-big-save-invite-sa-worlds-biggest-braai/)
- Castle Milk Stout's "Songs to Savour" tied the brand to Heritage Month through music (September 2025; headline only) — [Bizcommunity](https://www.bizcommunity.com/article/castle-milk-stout-revitalises-sa-heritage-with-songs-to-savour-954684a)
- Brutal Fruit's SheBeen put a women-only bar inside a Soweto tavern during Women's Month (August 2023), reclaiming the shebeen as a space for women — [Good Things Guy](https://www.goodthingsguy.com/in-pics-theres-a-new-shebeen-in-town-and-its-only-for-women/)
- Vernacular brand lines on record: Jana Swart's #ad Castle post, "Ons gaan nou braai!" and "having a cold one & watching sport" — [News24](https://www.news24.com/business/companies/ad-regulator-blasts-sab-in-landmark-influencer-ruling-20260728-1113); Hunter's "uk'fosta" / "Abafostayo" (meaning not given in the source; unverified gloss: street/township slang) — [Bizcommunity Uganda mirror](https://www.bizcommunity.ug/article/hunters-premium-cider-refreshes-the-ones-abafostayo-through-winter-903974a); Savanna's "Siyavanna" (appears to be an isiZulu-style pun on the brand name; unverified gloss) — [Bizcommunity](https://www.bizcommunity.com/Account/196/1/1201.html)
- Beach: the ARB treats the beach as an illegal drinking setting (Brutal Fruit ruling). The complainant's line "it is not necessary to drink alcohol to enjoy South African beaches" frames the public sentiment — [IOL, 6 Feb 2025](https://iol.co.za/news/south-africa/brutal-fruit-advert-condemned-for-promoting-illegal-beach-drinking-4a483eae-c8cc-4177-a207-f8c4521e5798)
- Corona localised its "beach" positioning for SA through sunsets and coastal place-names (V&A Amphitheatre OOH, seaside-gem bottles), not by showing drinking on the sand — [Bizcommunity: Hidden Sunsets](https://www.bizcommunity.ug/article/corona-flips-the-script-with-hidden-sunsets-ooh-campaign-471419a); [Bizcommunity: seaside bottles](https://www.bizcommunity.ug/article/limited-edition-corona-bottles-pay-tribute-to-south-african-seaside-gems-061035a)
- SAB times responsibility messaging to the festive season ("Drive Sharp", Oct 2024) — [Independent on Saturday](https://independentonsaturday.co.za/news/south-africa/western-cape/2024-10-18-sabs-festive-campaign-drive-sharp-encourages-responsible-drinking)

### Inferences
- For a cosmopolitan Cape Town tequila RTD, the respectful vernacular moves are:
  - **Bring-and-braai and Sunday lunch:** the can as a contribution to a shared table.
  - **Heritage Day:** celebrate people, food and music; don't put the can at the centre of "heritage".
  - **December and summer holidays:** pair with visible pacing and getting home safely.
  - **Beach and sunset:** use as mood, kept separate from product shots.
  - **Mixed groups at the fire:** a direct answer to the "men at the fire drinking beer" critique.
- "Cooler box" is good SA vernacular, but externally it should mean bringing something to share, not loading up on volume (Wildebeest "more = happier" caution).
- Use Afrikaans, isiZulu or township slang only where it is native to the speaker. The examples here come from brand-native puns (Siyavanna) or real influencers (Swart). A Mexican-heritage brand borrowing heavy local slang could read as appropriation. That is an inference, not tested against a source.

### Gaps
- Shisa nyama-specific alcohol brand campaigns (for example Hansa or Black Label at shisa nyamas) were not found in this pass.
- No source was found on how SA brands handle Sunday lunch or "bring and braai" in social captions specifically.
- No 2026 Heritage Day alcohol campaign coverage was found (Heritage Day 2026 was 24 September; coverage may not be indexed yet).
