# Alcohol-as-coping language vs. sociability language in alcohol marketing and social media (for Doña Fuego's social do's & don'ts)

Scope note: research date 16 October 2026. Primary sources were opened and quoted wherever possible (ASA/CAP rulings and AdviceOnline pages, the Portman Group guidance PDF, the Drinks Federation of South Africa / ARB code PDF, peer-reviewed papers on PMC/JMIR/Europe PMC). Where a claim rests only on a search-result snippet or an abstract fragment, it is marked **[snippet only]**. Anything not verified is marked **[unverified]**.

## 1. Drinking-motives evidence (Cooper's four-factor model): which motives predict harm, and why coping framing is the higher-risk register

### Takeaway
Across 30 years of research, *social* motives are the most common reason people drink and are tied mostly to moderate use, whereas *coping* motives (drinking to dull stress, worry or low mood) are the motive most consistently tied to alcohol problems, including problems over and above how much people drink; enhancement motives track heavy drinking. Copy that tells people they "need" a drink to get through stress speaks directly to the coping motive, the one the evidence flags as most harmful, while braai/beach/friends copy speaks to the social motive.

### Cited Findings
- **The foundational model.** Cooper, M. L. (1994). "Motivations for alcohol use among adolescents: Development and validation of a four-factor model." *Psychological Assessment*, 6(2), 117–128. doi:10.1037/1040-3590.6.2.117 (Crossref lists 1,796 citing works) — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1037/1040-3590.6.2.117)
- **The four motives as defined in the field's key review.** Kuntsche et al. describe them verbatim as "drinking to enhance positive mood or well-being (enhancement: positive, internal), to obtain social rewards (social: positive, external), to attenuate negative emotions (coping: negative, internal), and to avoid social rejection (conformity: negative, external)." — [Kuntsche, Knibbe, Gmel & Engels (2005), *Clinical Psychology Review* 25(7):841–861](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272735805000759)
- **Which motive goes with which outcome.** The same review (15 years of evidence on young people) found: "most young people reported drinking for social motives, some indicated enhancement motives, and only a few reported coping motives. Social motives appear to be associated with moderate alcohol use, enhancement with heavy drinking, and coping motives with alcohol-related problems." — [Kuntsche et al. 2005](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272735805000759)
- **Largest meta-analysis.** Bresin & Mekawi pooled 229 studies (254 samples; N = 130,705). Verbatim: "both enhancement and coping motives were the strongest predictors of drinking problems, but only enhancement motives were the strongest predictor of alcohol use." Nuance: "Enhancement and social motives were indirectly associated with alcohol use through drinking problems and with drinking problems through alcohol use, whereas coping motives were only indirectly associated with alcohol use through drinking problems." — [Bresin & Mekawi (2021), *Alcohol Clin Exp Res* 45(1):38–50, via Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:33206387&resultType=core&format=json)
- **Coping harms beyond the amount drunk (22,841 drinkers aged 13–16, 10 European countries).** "The direct effect, that is, the effect above and beyond alcohol use, indicated more negative consequences among those who tended to drink more frequently for coping motives… This is particularly important for coping drinkers because they are especially prone to adverse consequences over and above their alcohol use." Social, enhancement and coping motives all had positive *indirect* effects (via alcohol use) on injuries and academic problems. — [Wicki, Kuntsche et al. (2017), *Drug Alcohol Rev* 36(6):731–741, via Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:28580680&resultType=core&format=json)
- **Coping motives as "particularly hazardous".** "Drinking to cope, or endorsing strong coping motives for alcohol use, has been shown to be particularly hazardous." — [Grant et al. (2009), "Coping-anxiety and coping-depression motives predict different daily mood-drinking relationships", *Psychol Addict Behav*, doi:10.1037/a0015006](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015006) **[snippet only: abstract fragment from Firecrawl research index]**
- **Coping and consequences feed each other over time (young adult men).** "social and enhancement motives more strongly influenced alcohol use over time than the other way round. Coping motives predicted an increase in alcohol-related consequences, and vice versa." — [Reciprocal Influences of Drinking Motives… (2016), *Behav Med*, doi:10.1080/08964289.2016.1157057](https://doi.org/10.1080/08964289.2016.1157057) **[snippet only]**
- **Coping risk grows after the student years, i.e. in Doña Fuego's 25+ audience.** "drinking to cope (DTC) motivation becomes a greater risk factor for drinking-related problems as individuals progress through young adulthood." — [Post-college changes in the association between drinking motives and drinking-related problems (2020), *Addict Behav*, PMC7484277](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2020.106568) **[snippet only]**
- **Social motives not tied to problem drinking in a general adult sample.** "Coping, enhancement and conformity, but not social, motives were associated with problem alcohol consumption." — [*Drug Alcohol Rev* (2021), doi:10.1111/dar.13221](https://doi.org/10.1111/dar.13221) **[snippet only; authors not retrieved]**
- **Social is the most common motive across 13 countries.** "Social motives for drinking were most frequently indicated, followed by enhancement, coping, and conformity motives, in that order, in all age groups in all countries except Finland." — [Kuntsche et al. (2014), "Drinking motives and links to alcohol use in 13 European countries", *J Stud Alcohol Drugs*, doi:10.15288/jsad.2014.75.428](https://doi.org/10.15288/jsad.2014.75.428) **[snippet only]**
- **Drinking alone is the coping-shaped occasion.** A systematic review and meta-analysis examined "Associations between solitary drinking and increased alcohol consumption, alcohol problems, and drinking to cope motives in adolescents and young adults" (title). — [*Addiction* (2020), PMC8053066](https://doi.org/10.1111/add.15055) **[snippet only; effect sizes not retrieved]**
- **South African evidence: the weekend as an escape from stress.** 54 women in 12 alcohol-serving venues in Delft township (Cape Town), 2010–11: "Most women drank heavily and linked their drinking to stressors." Quotes: "I need to drink to calm me down"; "When the weekend comes and I drink my alcohol, I don't think about [my problems] anymore"; "If I'm at home then I will just think about my problems, that's why I go and drink over the weekends." Authors' conclusion: "Motivated attempts to use drinking as a coping strategy may maintain a cycle in which women experience stressors and then drink, only to experience further stressors and drink again." They also note "Relief is temporary" and drinking "can exacerbate stressors." — [Choi, Watt, MacFarlane, Sikkema, Skinner, Pieterse & Kalichman, "Drinking in the Context of Life Stressors: A Multidimensional Coping Strategy among South African Women", *Substance Use & Misuse* 49(1–2):66–76 (epub 2013)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4162844/)
- **Social media exposure and coping motives.** In college students, "Greater celebrity ARC [alcohol-related content] was linked to coping, enhancement, and conformity motives, and peak drinks." — ["Do you 'like' problems?…" (2023), *Drug Alcohol Rev*, PMC10838370](https://doi.org/10.1111/dar.13729) **[snippet only]**
- **2026 longitudinal work frames the mechanism.** "ARC often portrays drinking as rewarding and/or normative, potentially shaping…" (motives) — [Wang, Meshesha, Woerner et al. (2026), "Social media, drinking motives, and alcohol use in young adults: a longitudinal mediation study", *Addict Behav*, doi:10.1016/j.addbeh.2026.108799](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2026.108799) **[snippet only; results not retrieved]**

### Inferences
- The motive literature maps cleanly onto copy registers. "Get your Fuego fix", "you need this", "survive the week" and "erase today" are coping-motive messages (drink to reduce a negative state). "Bring a round to the braai" and "cheers to Saturday with the crew" are social-motive messages. The research gives a principled reason, not just a compliance one, to keep copy in the social register.
- Social framing is lower risk but not risk-free: Bresin & Mekawi and Wicki et al. both find social motives indirectly linked to harm through volume. Social copy therefore still needs to avoid volume cues ("cans deep", stacks of empties) and must not imply that alcohol *makes* the social occasion succeed (see Q3).
- Enhancement motives ("unleash your wild side", "get lit") predict heavy drinking in Bresin & Mekawi, so the safe register is "social + sensory/product truth", not "social + buzz".
- The Delft study is a heavy-drinking venue sample, not the general Checkers shopper. Its value for the one-pager is the local wording: the weekend as an escape ("When the weekend comes…") is exactly the frame that countdown-to-Friday content reproduces.

### Gaps
- No study was found that experimentally tests whether *exposure* to coping-framed brand copy raises viewers' coping motives. The causal link from marketing to coping motives is inferred from correlational work (celebrity ARC → coping motives) and regulators' reasoning, not shown by trials.
- Full text of the 2026 Wang et al. mediation study was not retrieved, so it is unknown whether coping motives mediated the effect of social media exposure in that sample.
- No South African population-level study of drinking motives (e.g. a DMQ-R survey of SA adults) was found within the tool budget.

## 2. Studies of marketing and social media content that frame alcohol as coping, self-care, reward or need (wine-mom culture, COVID-19 lockdown, Instagram, weekday framing, dependence vocabulary)

### Takeaway
Content analyses from the UK, Australia and Canada document brands and users presenting alcohol as a way to cope with lockdown, motherhood and "busy lives", in the vocabulary of self-care, reward, "me time" and treats. Researchers consistently flag this as problematic because it normalises drinking to cope. In contrast, ordinary young people's own alcohol posts are overwhelmingly *social* in content and earn more engagement when people are in the frame.

### Cited Findings
**COVID-19 lockdown (UK)**
- 18 leading brands on Facebook and Instagram, first UK lockdown (17 March–4 July 2020): "Alcohol use was also positioned as a way of coping (4%, n=31) with being confined to the home." — [Atkinson, Sumnall & Meadows (2021), "'We're in this together'…", *Int J Drug Policy* 98](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/15402/8/We%27re%20in%20this%20together%20A%20content%20analysis%20of%20marketing%20by%20alcohol%20brands%20on%20Facebook%20and%20Instagram%20during%20the%20first%20UK%20Lockdown%2C%202020.pdf)
- Verbatim examples from that study:
  - Bacardi: "When life gives us limes, we make #BACARDI Mojitos", hashtag "RaiseYourSpirits".
  - Barefoot and Freixenet urged followers to celebrate "little wins" and "victories" to "lift our spirits" by drinking.
  - Freixenet promoted "self-care"; BrewDog promoted "mindfulness".
  - Echo Falls: "There is some serious s**t going on right now. It may all seem super overwhelming but honestly it's ok to not be ok. Look out for your friends and family but don't forget yourself. So, if you fancy a glass of wine, or a G&T, please always do so responsibly. Stay safe love team EF x". The authors say this "associated self-care with alcohol use and normalised drinking as a coping mechanism".
  - Barefoot offered recipes to those "Feeling stir crazy?"
  - BrewDog: "HOW TO ENJOY A SUNNY WEEKEND: Step 1. Grab a beer. Step 2. Stay at home".

  — [Atkinson et al. 2021](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/15402/8/We%27re%20in%20this%20together%20A%20content%20analysis%20of%20marketing%20by%20alcohol%20brands%20on%20Facebook%20and%20Instagram%20during%20the%20first%20UK%20Lockdown%2C%202020.pdf)
- The same authors note that "Messages of togetherness underpinned the promotion of virtual interactions, for which alcohol use was presented as a key component". Togetherness framing can still be criticised when alcohol is made the key ingredient. — [Atkinson et al. 2021](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/15402/8/We%27re%20in%20this%20together%20A%20content%20analysis%20of%20marketing%20by%20alcohol%20brands%20on%20Facebook%20and%20Instagram%20during%20the%20first%20UK%20Lockdown%2C%202020.pdf)

**COVID-19 lockdown (Australia)**
- 42 alcohol and unhealthy food brands plus 12 parent companies, February–May 2020. "Approximately 5% to 10% of COVID-19-related posts across almost every category referred to the products helping people cope with isolation by creating a sense of normality or of the consumer deserving a treat in difficult times." "Isolation activities" made up 40–64% of COVID posts in the alcohol categories. The authors call this "'COVID-washing'… both common and concerning." — [Martino, Brooks, Browne, Carah et al. (2021), *JMIR Public Health Surveill* 7(3):e25202](https://publichealth.jmir.org/2021/3/e25202/)
- Same study: alcohol companies' mental-health posts were mainly reposted DrinkWise videos "featuring a celebrity doctor warning about the use of alcohol as a coping mechanism for stress during the COVID-19 pandemic". The paper cites bank-card data showing Australians "spent 46.2% more in bottle shops in the week ending on May 22, 2020" than in 2019. — [Martino et al. 2021](https://publichealth.jmir.org/2021/3/e25202/)
- Alcohol Change UK (blog by Dr Matt Parker, University of Portsmouth): "Lockdown may be stressful, and we know that stress increases cravings for alcohol". A survey found 72% drinking the same or less, "However, those statistics still leave 28% drinking more"; frontline staff reported "relapse of some people who were in recovery". — [Alcohol Change UK, "Drinking to cope? Stress, boredom and alcohol use during COVID-19 lockdown"](https://alcoholchange.org.uk/blog/drinking-to-cope-stress-boredom-and-alcohol-use-during-covid-19-lockdown)
- University of Cambridge summary of later research: "COVID-19 and lockdown measures drove some individuals more than others to use alcohol to cope with stress". — [University of Cambridge news, "One in three adults drank more alcohol during first lockdown"](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/one-in-three-adults-drank-more-alcohol-during-first-lockdown) **[snippet only]**

**Wine-mom / "mommy juice" culture**
- Reflexive thematic analysis of 40 Instagram #winemom posts. Verbatim tropes include "'wine mom' or 'mommy juice'", "'mommy's sippy cup' and 'wine o'clock'", "'Wine Wednesday'" and boxed wine shown next to a child's juice box. Conclusion: "alcohol was presented as the saviour that allowed them to cope with their domestic duties". These posts "problematically frame alcohol as the solution"; wine is "presented as recreational and taking the edge off… as part of routine self-care, and as being a necessary way to cope with being a modern mother". The authors warn that wine-mom culture "hides or masks problematic drinking". — [Harding, Whittingham & McGannon (2021), "#sendwine: An Analysis of Motherhood, Alcohol Use and #winemom Culture on Instagram", *Substance Abuse: Research and Treatment* 15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8114293/)
- A 2024 APA record describes "Wine mom" culture as one "which encourages mothers to drink alcohol to cope with stressors associated with motherhood" that "has become mainstream in popular media". — ["Wine mom culture: Investigating social media influence on mothers' alcohol norms" (2024), PsycNet](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2024-67159-001) **[snippet only; authors not retrieved]**

**Women-targeted brand marketing: "me time", reward and self-care**
- In Facebook and Instagram posts by UK brands, a theme labelled "'On the go' and 'Me time': alcohol use as time out and reward from working and domestic roles". Examples:
  - "mini" and "in a can" formats framed as "perfect to pop in your bag ready to enjoy on-the-go!"
  - A poll asking which item women "couldn't you live without?" (lipstick, alcohol, chocolate or phone).
  - Echo Falls: "Anything is possible with a little lipstick and prosecco…you've got this queen".

  The authors conclude that self-care discourses "instructed women to reward themselves to time out and relaxation through drinking… As a result, alcohol use was encouraged as a feminine way of dealing with stress, which is problematic when the link between alcohol use and mental health is considered." — [Atkinson, Meadows, Emslie, Lyons & Sumnall, "'Pretty in Pink' and 'Girl Power'…", *Int J Drug Policy* (2022), PDF hosted by Movendi](https://movendi.ngo/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/1-s2.0-S0955395921004655-main.pdf); [PubMed 34906847](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34906847/) (volume/article number **[unverified]**)

**Young adults' own posts are social**
- 192 young people (mean age 20.6) on Facebook and Instagram. Alcohol posts "frequently depict alcohol in a positive social context (425/438, 97.0%) and display people holding drinks (277/412, 67.2%)". 54.2% were placed on timelines by others through tagging. Social posts drew more likes than posts with no people visible (mean 35.50 vs 10.34, P<.001). — [Hendriks, Van den Putte, Gebhardt & Moreno (2018), *J Med Internet Res* 20(6):e226](https://www.jmir.org/2018/6/e226/)

**Weekday, clock and "skip to the good part" framing (rulings detailed in Q3)**
- ASA examples of weekday or clock framing:
  - Diageo's "Wednesday. I'm declaring war on mid-week boredom" (2014) was found to suggest drinking alleviates boredom.
  - Jack Daniel's "Shorter days mean we can skip to the good part" (2024) "implied that the rest of the day was tedious and to be endured until drinking time began".

  — [ASA/CAP AdviceOnline, "Alcohol: Boredom and loneliness"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-boredom-and-loneliness.html)

**Dependence and drug-culture vocabulary**
- An ASA-ruled meme read "People are not addicted to alcohol or drugs, they are addicted to escaping reality". Another read "I see people all the time going to therapy, thinking they've got a problem… order a Crateman and live a little...or a lot". Both breached the Code (Drink Doctor Ltd, 24 May 2017). — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Boredom and loneliness"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-boredom-and-loneliness.html)
- A wine-label sticker read "PRESCRIPTION ALCOHOL… DOSE: Drink one 175ml dose twice daily. If symptoms persist, take double the dose stated…". The ASA upheld the complaint because it "linked alcohol to medicine" (Novielo London, 7 June 2023). — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Therapeutic claims"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)
- South African statutory label warnings include "Alcohol is addictive" (one of the warnings listed in the DF-SA code §1.6.2.6). — [Drinks Federation of South Africa, Alcohol Industry Communications Code of Conduct (ARB version, Mar 2024)](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct_-marketing-and-communications-2023---arb-version-final-mar-2024.pdf)

### Inferences
- Coping framing rarely looks like "drink to cope". It arrives dressed as self-care, a "little win", "me time", a treat you deserve, a survival kit, or a joke meme. A do's-and-don'ts sheet should therefore list these disguises, not just the obvious words.
- "Fix" belongs to the same family as "dose", "prescription" and "addicted". In everyday English it means a dose of an addictive drug or a craving satisfied (common usage; no dictionary source was retrieved). It frames the product as something the drinker is hooked on and needs, which is the dependence and coping reading regulators object to (Q3). In South Africa it would also sit beside a mandatory label warning that "Alcohol is addictive", so a "fix" line contradicts the can it promotes.
- Hendriks et al. show the social register is the native, high-engagement register of alcohol content (97% positive-social; three times the likes when people are in shot). Doña Fuego loses nothing in reach by staying social instead of using coping hooks. This is an inference about engagement, not a tested claim about brand posts.
- The weekday clock is a coping device in disguise. "Friday can't come soon enough", "survived Monday" and "it's 5 o'clock somewhere" all imply that the hours before drinking are something to endure. The Jack Daniel's ruling turned on exactly that implication.

### Gaps
- No verbatim brand "quarantini" post was found in a peer-reviewed source within the tool budget. The term is widely reported but **[unverified]** here as a brand-used hashtag.
- No content analysis specific to "Hump day" or "Monday" alcohol brand posts was found. The evidence for weekday framing comes from ASA rulings, not prevalence studies.
- No peer-reviewed study was found that counts addiction vocabulary ("fix", "hooked", "addicted", "craving", "can't function without") in alcohol brand posts. The evidence here is rulings and examples, not prevalence data.
- No South African content analysis of brand posts during the 2020–21 sales bans was found.

## 3. How codes and regulators treat specific words and devices (fix, need, therapy, medicine, cure, self-care, treat yourself, earned it, liquid courage, survive, dose, wine o'clock, day drinking, hair of the dog), with rulings

### Takeaway
UK and South African codes do not ban single words. They ban *implications*: that alcohol is needed, can relieve stress or change mood, can solve problems or boredom, has curative or medicinal properties, boosts confidence, or makes an occasion succeed. Rulings show that humour, memes, hashtags and metaphor do not excuse the implication, and that context decides. "Day drinking" with one drink in a relaxed scene passed; "One to erase today" and "A survival kit, if you will" failed.

### Cited Findings
**The rules (verbatim)**
- **CAP Code (UK non-broadcast), s.18:**
  - 18.6: "Marketing communications must not imply that alcohol might be indispensable or take priority in life or that drinking alcohol can overcome boredom, loneliness or other problems."
  - 18.7: "Marketing communications must not imply that alcohol has therapeutic qualities. Alcohol must not be portrayed as capable of changing mood, physical condition or behaviour or as a source of nourishment."
  - Also in s.18: "Marketing communications must not claim or imply that alcohol can enhance confidence or popularity."
  - Also in s.18: "Marketing communications must not imply that drinking alcohol is a key component of the success of a personal relationship or social event. The consumption of alcohol may be portrayed as sociable or thirst-quenching."
  - 18.1 adds: "Care should be taken not to exploit the young, the immature or those who are mentally or socially vulnerable."

  The confidence and social-event rules are probably 18.2 and 18.3; those numbers are inferred from the order on the page. — [CAP Code section 18](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/18.html)
- **CAP guidance on "need":** marketers must not "imply that anyone might need (as opposed to merely enjoy) an alcoholic drink". — [ASA/CAP AdviceOnline, "Alcohol: Therapeutic claims" (20 May 2026)](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)
- **CAP guidance on stress:** "featuring or encouraging those who are clearly stressed or more anxious than normal to drink alcohol is likely to be interpreted as saying that alcohol is necessary for relaxation, has therapeutic qualities or mental health benefits – all of which are likely to breach rule 18.7". — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Therapeutic claims"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)
- **CAP guidance on mental health:** "Alcohol marketers must not imply that consuming alcohol can improve one's mental health, suggest that alcohol is necessary for good mental health, or suggest that alcohol can provide emotional support." — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Therapeutic claims"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)
- **CAP guidance on "chill":** "Claims such as 'chill with your mates' may be acceptable depending on context – however… if the ad implies alcohol can make drinkers more 'chilled', the claim could be the wrong side of the line." Implying alcohol is a sedative or "in some other way calming" is "problematic". — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Therapeutic claims"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)
- **Portman Group Code rule 3.2(j):** "A drink, its packaging or promotion should not suggest that the product has therapeutic qualities, can enhance mental or physical capabilities, or change mood or behaviour." "Any direct or implied suggestion that consumption of alcohol has helped the consumer relax is likely to be unacceptable under the Code." — [Portman Group, Therapeutic Qualities rule page](https://www.portmangroup.org.uk/rule_category/therapeutic-qualities/)
- **Portman Advisory Service guidance note on 3.2(j), verbatim bullets:**
  - "Do not claim that anyone needs, as opposed to likes, or enjoys, a drink."
  - "Alcohol must not be the catalyst for change of mood or mental state."
  - "The portrayal of drinking as a legitimate accompaniment to a relaxing setting will be acceptable… where the relaxation element is attributed to one's surroundings/setting, and not the alcohol".
  - Activities "which suggest that they can help consumers to 'forget' their everyday lives… are likely to be problematic".
  - "suggestions that a drink can invoke feelings/emotions… for example 'get in touch with your wild side', are likely to be problematic".

  — [Portman Group guidance note, Rule 3.2(j) (PDF)](https://portmangroup21.wpenginepowered.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Rule-3.2j-Therapeutic-qualities-enhancing-mental-and-or-physical-capabilities-and-change-in-mood-or-behaviour.pdf)
- **South Africa: Drinks Federation of South Africa (DF-SA) Alcohol Industry Communications Code, enforced by the ARB:**
  - §1.4: Commercial Communications "should not imply that drinking resulted in business, social, professional, academic, athletic, financial, or other personal success, nor that drinking can solve personal problems, overcome inhibitions, shyness or social barriers, or enable social acceptance."
  - §1.6.4: they "may not claim that alcohol has curative qualities, or offer alcohol as a performance enhancer, stimulant, sedative, or tranquiliser."
  - §1.7.2: they "may not portray or in any way endorse or glamourise illicit drugs, drug culture…"
  - §1.9.1 prohibits brand names or packaging that convey "the intoxicating effect of alcohol, or that use colloquialisms for alcohol (e.g., 'dop,' 'booze,' or 'grog'), illegal activity, alcohol abuse… illicit drugs or drug culture".

  — [DF-SA Code (ARB version, March 2024)](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct_-marketing-and-communications-2023---arb-version-final-mar-2024.pdf)
- **EU benchmark:** the EU broadcasting rule (TWF/AVMS Directive) states that alcohol advertising "shall not claim that alcohol has therapeutic qualities or that it is a stimulant, a sedative or a means of resolving personal conflicts". — [European Commission, "The Impact of Alcohol Advertising" (2004 project report)](https://ec.europa.eu/health/ph_projects/2004/action3/docs/2004_3_16_frep_a4_en.pdf) **[snippet only]**

**Rulings, grouped by word family**
- **"Self-care", "emotional support", "erase today" (coping and therapy).** La Vieille Ferme TikTok, upheld 20 May 2026. The ad showed three wine boxes labelled "One to resurrect my soul", "One for emotional support" and "One to erase today", with the caption "My health? Depends on all three [melting face emoji] #chickenwine #lavieilleferme #fyp #selfcare" and a "Finding Balance: Emotional Support and Self-Care" text. The ASA found that "One to erase today" "positioned alcohol as a way to cope with or distract from difficult times and to help deal with the stress of a hard day". It also read the line as "drinking to the point of memory loss", reinforced by visuals of "someone filling their glass repeatedly". The self-care text "would likely be interpreted as suggesting that drinking the wine formed part of a self-care and well-being routine." Breached CAP 18.1, 18.7 and 18.17; TikTok removed the ad. — [ASA ruling, Perrin et Fils SAS t/a La Vieille Ferme](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/perrin-et-fils-sas-g26-1334485-perrin-et-fils-sas.html)
- **"Got your back" (beer as solution to life's problems).** BrewDog Wingman, upheld 6 August 2025. Copy: "BREWDOG. ALWAYS GOT YOUR BACK… Some things in life go AWOL – WiFi fails, the weather turns hostile… But Wingman? Wingman stands firm." The ASA found that "the scenarios presented were real-life problems… and the beer was a solution to overcoming those problems". — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Therapeutic claims"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)
- **"Survive" / "survival kit".** Reserve Ltd, 10 March 2021. Email subject "Friend, forget Dry January", body text: "…we thought you might need all the help you could get over the next few weeks, so we've put together Lockdown 3.0 - a 6-bottle mixed case… A survival kit, if you will." The ASA said "the overall impression of the ad was that alcohol could overcome problems, including boredom and low mood", even though non-alcoholic options were mentioned. — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Boredom and loneliness"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-boredom-and-loneliness.html)
- **"Care less" humour.** Blackrose Ltd, 11 December 2019. A Facebook post read "If at first you don't succeed, try drinking a bottle of prosecco, you'll be surprised at how much less you'll care". This breached 18.7 because it "suggested that prosecco could help one forget their problems". CAP adds that "humour does not invalidate the need to comply with the Code." — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Therapeutic claims"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)
- **Mood vocabulary.** Drake & Morgan, 9 October 2019. A quiz offered a "potion to match their emotion", asked people whether they were "overstretched", "low-spirited" or "walking on sunshine", and used "give yourself a boost". Upheld. — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Therapeutic claims"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)
- **Confidence and "wild side" (the "liquid courage" family).**
  - Wild Africa Cream Liqueur's "Unleash your wild side" was upheld as implying the drink "changed a woman's mood and enhanced her confidence" (1 July 2009). — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Therapeutic claims"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)
  - In the 2023 Muff Liquor Co. influencer video, Laura Whitmore danced "more confidently and enthusiastically" after switching to the alcoholic drink, under the caption "If drinks were dance moves". The ASA found this implied enhanced confidence and mood and that "sobriety was boring". — [RPC summary of ASA ruling (31 March 2023)](https://www.rpclegal.com/snapshots/advertising-and-marketing/spring-2023/asa-slams-social-media-post-for-breaching-rules-on-alcohol-advertising/)
- **Stimulant / "rush".** In 2000 the ASA upheld a complaint against "vodka rush" because it "implied alcohol could increase drinkers' energy levels". — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Therapeutic claims"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)
- **Medicine, cure, dose, tonic.**
  - Portman Panel 2012, Dr Von Hyde's Herbal Liqueur: "cure for all", "a healer", "medication for the masses" found in breach.
  - Portman Panel 2012, Vitesse Noir: "just when you are not sure you can manage the last dance, take a nip and it'll be just the tonic that you needed" upheld.

  — [Portman 3.2(j) guidance PDF](https://portmangroup21.wpenginepowered.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Rule-3.2j-Therapeutic-qualities-enhancing-mental-and-or-physical-capabilities-and-change-in-mood-or-behaviour.pdf)
  - Novielo "PRESCRIPTION ALCOHOL… DOSE" (2023), upheld: [ASA/CAP](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)
- **Indispensable / "can't live without".** Hi-Spirits (Fireball), 17 July 2013: "I'd dump my boyfriend for more Fireball cinnamon whisky" and "The only thing in life more important to me than Fireball is…". The ASA rejected the defence that these were jokes. — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Boredom and loneliness"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-boredom-and-loneliness.html)
- **Weekday boredom and clock-watching.** Diageo, 23 July 2014: "Wednesday. I'm declaring war on mid-week boredom". Brown-Forman / Jack Daniel's, 28 February 2024: "Shorter days mean we can skip to the good part"; the ASA rejected the advertiser's "spending more time with friends" defence. — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Boredom and loneliness"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-boredom-and-loneliness.html)
- **"Day drinking" and "mid-week drinking": NOT upheld, because context mattered.** Served Drinks (2022):
  - "Nothing better than day dreaming and day drinking … your ultimate day drinking partner" passed. The woman "was shown with one drink in her hand and no other drinks were visible… There was no suggestion that she was using alcohol as a means of escaping her responsibilities, such as work."
  - "Mid-week drinking season has arrived" passed, as a reference to "mid-week social events during the festive season".
  - The same advertiser's email "Forget Dry January… Is dry January becoming a little dry?" WAS upheld under 18.6 (drinking to overcome boredom).
  - Its influencer posts "you love a drink but also enjoy an active lifestyle" and "I also really care about my well-being" were upheld as prohibited health claims.

  — [ASA ruling, Served Drinks Ltd](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/served-drinks-ltd-g22-1146766-served-drinks-ltd.html); [The Spirits Business, July 2022](https://www.thespiritsbusiness.com/2022/07/ellie-gouldings-served-ads-banned/)
- **Therapeutic activities without a causal link: NOT upheld.** Bombay Sapphire showed artists making artwork. Because the artists were "already… creatively minded" and were not shown drinking before, during or after the work, there was no implied link (Bacardi Global Brands, 8 January 2020). — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Therapeutic claims"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)
- **Seasonal "get the summer started".** Portman's Advisory Service (12 June 2026): "Lines such as 'get the summer started' could be interpreted as a suggestion that consumption of the drink could improve the success of the summer season. We would instead recommend that marketing lines focus on the quality and taste of the drink, for example 'a refreshing choice for your summer drinks'." Also: "Presenting a chilled drink as a serving suggestion is acceptable, but care should be taken to avoid any suggestion that a chilled drink could make a consumer feel 'chilled' or relaxed." — [Portman Group, "A summertime guide to responsible alcohol marketing"](https://www.portmangroup.org.uk/a-summertime-guide-to-responsible-alcohol-marketing/)

### Inferences
Word-by-word read-across for Doña Fuego. Each line is an inference from the rules and rulings above, not a ruling on that exact word.
- **"Fix" ("Get your Fuego fix"):** implies need or dependence (Portman: "needs, as opposed to likes"; CAP: "need (as opposed to merely enjoy)"). It borrows drug-culture slang (DF-SA §1.7.2 "drug culture") and reads as satisfying a craving, i.e. a mood change (CAP 18.7, Portman 3.2(j)). High risk under all three codes, and on-brand only for the wrong reason.
- **"Need" / "you need this" / "tag someone who needs this":** explicitly covered by the Portman and CAP "need vs enjoy" guidance. Swap to "who's bringing the…", "tag your braai crew" or "tag who you're sharing with".
- **"Therapy", "medicine", "cure", "dose", "prescription", "tonic", "remedy", "healer":** covered by therapeutic and curative bans (CAP 18.7; Portman 3.2(j); DF-SA §1.6.4), with direct precedents (Novielo, Dr Von Hyde's, Vitesse Noir).
- **"Self-care", "me time", "emotional support", "mental health day":** La Vieille Ferme (2026) is the leading precedent. Avoid entirely.
- **"Treat yourself" / "you deserve it" / "you've earned it":** no ruling turns on these exact words, but Martino et al. classed "deserving a treat in difficult times" as a coping theme, and Atkinson et al. tie "reward" to self-care coping. Risky when paired with a hard day or week; safer only when the "treat" is the occasion or the flavour and there is no hardship set-up. Best avoided in favour of "Saturday sorted", "the good stuff" or "made for sharing".
- **"Survive the week" / "survival kit" / "made it to Friday":** the Reserve "survival kit" ruling plus the 18.6 "other problems" wording make this a clear don't.
- **"Liquid courage" / "shot of confidence" / "unleash your wild side":** CAP's confidence rule, DF-SA §1.4 ("overcome inhibitions, shyness"), Wild Africa and Muff Liquor. Don't.
- **"Wine o'clock" / "it's 5 o'clock somewhere" / "skip to the good part" / countdown-to-Friday:** clock-watching implies the day is to be endured until drinking time (Jack Daniel's 2024; Diageo 2014). Don't.
- **"Day drinking":** not automatically a breach (Served). It depends on one drink, a relaxed setting and no escape-from-responsibility cue. Usable with care, but "Saturday afternoon" or "long lunch" is safer and on-occasion.
- **"Hair of the dog" / hangover cure:** no alcohol-specific ruling found. By inference it is a curative or therapeutic claim (DF-SA §1.6.4 "curative qualities"; CAP 18.7 "physical condition") and also implies the previous night's excess. Don't.
- **"Chill" / "ice-cold":** "ice-cold" and "serve chilled" are serving suggestions and acceptable (Portman 2026). "Chill out with a Fuego" crosses into mood change.
- **"Get the party started" / "makes the braai":** social-success risk (CAP's social-event rule; DF-SA §1.4; Portman's 2026 "get the summer started" advice). Keep the braai as the hero and the can as the accompaniment.
- **7% ABV as a selling point:** the DF-SA code says "Non-label forms of Commercial Communications should not feature or depict a product's alcohol content unless the alcohol content by volume is at or below 2.5%" (§1.5.2) and "Advertising of alcohol content is not permitted" (§1.5.3). CAP also bars implying a drink "may be preferred because of its alcohol content or intoxicating effect". So "7% kick" or "packs a punch" copy is a don't. — [DF-SA Code](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct_-marketing-and-communications-2023---arb-version-final-mar-2024.pdf); [CAP s.18](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/18.html)

### Gaps
- No ARB (South Africa) ruling was found that turns specifically on coping, stress or "fix" wording. Searching the ARB rulings database directly was outside the tool budget, so SA precedent on these words is unknown, not absent.
- No ASA or Portman ruling was found that turns on the exact words "fix", "craving", "hooked", "treat yourself", "you've earned it", "wine o'clock", "it's 5 o'clock somewhere" or "hair of the dog". The read-across above is inference from the rules and the nearest precedents.
- The CAP rule numbers for the confidence and social-event rules (likely 18.2 and 18.3) were inferred from page order, not read from the page.
- The full text of the EU AVMS Directive article (Art. 22) was not opened; that wording comes from a secondary EC report.

## 4. Recovery and public-health perspective: why addiction and coping language in alcohol marketing is a concern (Alcohol Change UK, Movendi, SANCA, Aware.org.za)

### Takeaway
Public-health and recovery voices object to coping and self-care framing because stress raises cravings and drives relapse, because "funny" coping content hides problem drinking, and because it targets people who are already struggling. In South Africa, SANCA's 2026 messaging centres exactly on alcohol "gradually becom[ing] a way of coping". Even the industry-linked Aware.org.za warns about the "I deserve a drink after today" mindset.

### Cited Findings
- **Alcohol Change UK (Parker):** "stress increases cravings for alcohol"; "social isolation and lack of access to health services are among the most significant risk factors for mental health problems, including drug and alcohol misuse and dependence". Frontline teams reported "relapse of some people who were in recovery". — [Alcohol Change UK blog](https://alcoholchange.org.uk/blog/drinking-to-cope-stress-boredom-and-alcohol-use-during-covid-19-lockdown)
- **Masking problem drinking (wine-mom researchers):** "the problem with the cultural perception of the wine mom is that it hides or masks problematic drinking". (Meant to be) funny posts "reinforce the message that women's drinking is not a problem, but rather an amusing pastime to cope with the demands of womanhood and motherhood". "Mental health literature has aptly identified that alcohol should not be used as a means for coping." — [Harding et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8114293/)
- **SANCA (South African National Council on Alcoholism and Drug Dependence), Women's Month post, 27 August 2026:** "For some women, alcohol or other substances may gradually become a way of coping. What begins occasionally can develop into harmful patterns that affect physical and emotional wellbeing, relationships, work and family life." "A woman may continue working, caring for her children and meeting her responsibilities while struggling privately." "Substance use disorders require understanding, appropriate intervention and support — not shame." — [SANCA, "Behind the Strength: Women, Stress and Substance Use"](https://www.sancanational.info/post/behind-the-strength-women-stress-and-substance-use)
- **SANCA's 2026 calendar** also includes an OcSober challenge ("31 days without alcohol") and a piece titled "April, Alcohol & Awareness: Why Binge Drinking Spikes — and How to Stay in Control", which links long weekends and holidays to binge spikes. — [SANCA blog](https://www.sancanational.info/blog) (April piece **[snippet only]**)
- **Aware.org.za** (Association for Alcohol Responsibility and Education; mandate "to promote responsible drinking by adults who choose to consume alcohol and reduce its harmful use") posted a video captioned: "I deserve a drink after today? Sometimes that's fine. But when alcohol becomes the default way to deal with stress, it can quietly affect sleep, mood and long-term health." — [Aware.org.za Facebook video](https://www.facebook.com/aware.org.za/videos/i-deserve-a-drink-after-todaysometimes-thats-fine-but-when-alcohol-becomes-the-d/943179725231084/) **[snippet only: caption text from search result; video not opened]**; [Aware.org.za home](https://www.aware.org.za/)
- **Aware.org.za's ties to industry:** the DF-SA industry code directs that delivery vehicles "display the Authorised Responsible Drinking Message and the website of Aware.org" (§1.8.3). — [DF-SA Code](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct_-marketing-and-communications-2023---arb-version-final-mar-2024.pdf) (Aware.org's funding model **[unverified]** here)
- **Movendi International** summarises research showing "the alcohol industry's 'responsible drinking' messaging aligns precisely with postfeminist healthism ideals that women have…" — [Movendi Science Digest](https://movendi.ngo/science-digest/how-the-alcohol-industrys-responsible-use-messaging-exploits-womens-ideals-of-empowerment-and-wellbeing-and-keeps-them-blaming-themselves/) **[snippet only]**. Movendi also reported in 2020 that "the alcohol industry is exploiting the current public health crisis as a marketing opportunity" — [Movendi, "USA: Adults Turn to Alcohol to Cope With COVID-19"](http://movendi.ngo/policy-updates/2020/05/15/usa-adults-turn-to-alcohol-to-cope-with-covid-19/) **[snippet only]**
- **The "vulnerable" clause in the UK code:** CAP 18.1 requires that "Care should be taken not to exploit the young, the immature or those who are mentally or socially vulnerable." — [CAP s.18](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/18.html)
- **The local lived experience of the cycle (Delft):** "the reason why I am drinking is all of the hurt I had to endure in my life"; women said they "must" drink on the weekends "despite having to borrow money or sacrifice basic expenditures to do so". — [Choi et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4162844/)

### Inferences
- Recovery voices care less about whether a line is "legal" than about who reads it. A follower who is drinking to cope, or in early recovery, reads "get your fix" or "survive the week" as validation. Coping language most persuades the people it harms most (a synthesis of Alcohol Change UK, Harding et al. and CAP 18.1's vulnerability clause).
- In South Africa, SANCA's current framing ("when coping becomes a concern", stigma, women "struggling privately") makes coping copy reputationally risky even where no ARB complaint follows. A brand line built on "No BS" honesty jars with copy that winks at dependence.
- Aware.org.za's own creative ("Sometimes that's fine. But when alcohol becomes the default way to deal with stress…") shows the SA industry-linked body treating "I deserve a drink after today" as the start of a slippery slope. That is a useful, non-preachy local reference point for the social media manager.

### Gaps
- No Alcohol Change UK, IAS or Movendi document was found that specifically discusses *addiction vocabulary* ("fix", "hooked", "addicted") in alcohol advertising. The concern is documented for coping, self-care and humour framing generally.
- No IAS (Institute of Alcohol Studies) or WHO Europe report on digital alcohol marketing was opened within the tool budget. Their positions are not represented here.
- Aware.org.za's funding and governance were not verified from a primary source.
- No SANCA statement specifically on alcohol *advertising* language was found. SANCA's quoted material is about coping and stigma generally.

## 5. The positive alternative vocabulary: occasion and ritual, sensory and product truth, people and place; moderation without lecturing

### Takeaway
The codes do not just prohibit; they spell out what is allowed. Drinking may be shown as "sociable or thirst-quenching" (CAP) and as "a relaxed, sociable, and enjoyable part of a well-lived life" (DF-SA). Relaxation may be credited to the *setting*, not the drink (Portman). Regulators actively steer marketers to "taste and quality". That gives Doña Fuego a clear, compliant register: the braai, the beach and the people are the heroes; the can is the accompaniment; the product truth (real tequila, real lemon and lime) carries the emotion. Moderation messaging works best framed as cool, social and self-directed (Heineken's model), not as warnings.

### Cited Findings
- **CAP permission:** "The consumption of alcohol may be portrayed as sociable or thirst-quenching." — [CAP s.18](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/18.html)
- **DF-SA permission (South Africa):** "Commercial Communications that depict responsible drinking among adults as a relaxed, sociable, and enjoyable part of a well-lived life has a role to play in reinforcing a responsible approach to drinking" (§1.2). — [DF-SA Code](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct_-marketing-and-communications-2023---arb-version-final-mar-2024.pdf)
- **Portman on setting vs drink:** "The portrayal of drinking as a legitimate accompaniment to a relaxing setting will be acceptable… expressions where the relaxation element is attributed to one's surroundings/setting, and not the alcohol, which is a by-product of the setting, are likely to be acceptable." — [Portman 3.2(j) guidance](https://portmangroup21.wpenginepowered.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Rule-3.2j-Therapeutic-qualities-enhancing-mental-and-or-physical-capabilities-and-change-in-mood-or-behaviour.pdf)
- **Portman on taste and quality:** "References to the taste and quality of a product are likely to be fine under the Code and the Advisory Service would encourage producers to focus on these elements when making emotive statements." — [Portman 3.2(j) guidance](https://portmangroup21.wpenginepowered.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Rule-3.2j-Therapeutic-qualities-enhancing-mental-and-or-physical-capabilities-and-change-in-mood-or-behaviour.pdf)
- **Portman's model swap (2026):** "get the summer started" → "a refreshing choice for your summer drinks". "Presenting a chilled drink as a serving suggestion is acceptable." Marketing lines that place "alcohol as a legitimate accompaniment to a social occasion are acceptable". — [Portman summertime guide](https://www.portmangroup.org.uk/a-summertime-guide-to-responsible-alcohol-marketing/)
- **CAP on "chill with your mates":** "may be acceptable depending on context". — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Therapeutic claims"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)
- **Beach and pool caution (Portman 2026):** beach and pool scenes are acceptable "so long as care is taken to avoid any images that suggest the drink has been consumed before or during taking part in water sports or swimming". Avoid accessories with under-18 appeal, such as "buckets and spades or inflatables with child-friendly themes". — [Portman summertime guide](https://www.portmangroup.org.uk/a-summertime-guide-to-responsible-alcohol-marketing/)
- **Product truth without health claims:**
  - DF-SA §1.6.3 bars terms like "healthier", "better for you" or "safer" and any implied health benefit, while labels may give factual calorie and carbohydrate information.
  - CAP and Portman allow "factual information about product contents" but no "health, fitness or weight-control claims".
  - In the Served ruling, "I also really care about my well-being" was upheld as a health claim.

  — [DF-SA Code](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct_-marketing-and-communications-2023---arb-version-final-mar-2024.pdf); [CAP s.18](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/18.html); [ASA Served ruling](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/served-drinks-ltd-g22-1146766-served-drinks-ltd.html)
- **Non-alc Margarita: brand-name proviso.** DF-SA §1.10.4 lets alcohol-free drinks be promoted "as a sociable option for avoiding over-consuming alcohol" and for occasions like driving, but only "provided that the beverages do not carry an alcohol brand name". Under §1.10.3, alcohol-free products that do carry the brand "shall comply with all requirements of this Code… for alcohol-branded Commercial Communications". §1.10.2: alcohol-free and non-alcohol drinks "may only be promoted and sold to people of legal drinking age". — [DF-SA Code](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct_-marketing-and-communications-2023---arb-version-final-mar-2024.pdf)
- **No disparaging non-drinkers:** DF-SA §1.2.5 says communications "should make it clear that drinking is a personal choice and never disparage people who choose to abstain from drinking or to consume alcohol responsibly". The ASA has repeatedly ruled against "sobriety is boring" and "Forget Dry January" framing (Muff Liquor; Served; Reserve). — [DF-SA Code](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct_-marketing-and-communications-2023---arb-version-final-mar-2024.pdf); [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Boredom and loneliness"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-boredom-and-loneliness.html)
- **Heineken's moderation-as-cool model:**
  - "'Moderate Drinkers Wanted' is the third chapter in Heineken®'s ongoing commitment to making moderate drinking cool."
  - Earlier chapters: 2011's "Sunrise" ("there are no limits, when you know your limits") and 2014's "Dance More, Drink Slow" with DJ Armin van Buuren.
  - Heineken said it invests "10% of the brand's media spend in dedicated responsible consumption campaigns".
  - The brand cited research that "69% of millennials cite avoiding loss of control as the primary motivation for limiting alcohol consumption on a night-out".

  — [HEINEKEN press release, 13 January 2016](https://www.theheinekencompany.com/newsroom/heineken-launches-new-global-responsible-consumption-campaign/)
- **Criticism of the Heineken framing:** the 2016 spot carried "the clear message that women prefer men to be moderate drinkers" (Heineken), and the press framed it as an ad that "suggests women prefer men who don't drink too much". — [The Guardian, 13 January 2016](https://www.theguardian.com/media/2016/jan/13/heineken-ad-moderate-drinking) **[snippet only]**; [MediaPost, "Heineken Stands By Its 'Moderate Drinkers Wanted' Campaign"](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/266941/heineken-stands-by-its-moderate-drinkers-wanted.html) **[snippet only]**
- **Social content is what engages:** posts with people visible earned mean 35.50 likes vs 10.34 for posts without people. — [Hendriks et al. 2018](https://www.jmir.org/2018/6/e226/)

### Inferences
Draft vocabulary bank for the one-pager. Each item is grounded in the permissions above (sociable, thirst-quenching, setting-attributed, taste and quality, serving suggestion); none is individually cleared by a regulator.
- **Occasion and ritual (social-motive register):** bring, share, pour, toast, cheers, crack open, pass one over, cooler box, ice bucket, the braai, the beach, the long Saturday lunch, the afternoon with the crew, "who's bringing the cooler box?", "one for the table", "a round of Palomas for the crew". Caveat: "round" can read as successive drinks. "Share" is the safest default.
- **Sensory and product truth:** real tequila, real lemon and lime juice, ice-cold, serve chilled, salt rim, a squeeze of lime, grapefruit (Paloma), a bit of chilli heat (Spicy Margarita), zesty, tangy, refreshing, crisp, thirst-quenching (CAP-permitted word). Keep "No BS" as the honesty claim about ingredients. Do not stretch it into "guilt-free", "clean" or "better for you" (DF-SA §1.6.3; Served).
- **People and place:** the crew, mates, your people, the host, the tannie who brings the salads, the braai master, a Saturday at the coast, the stoep, the cooler box on the sand (not in the water: Portman beach caution). Let the setting carry the relaxation ("Saturday at the coast, sorted") rather than the drink ("Fuego helps you unwind").
- **Moderation without lecturing:** borrow Heineken's "make moderation cool" logic, not its "women will like you" logic, which brushes against social-success and attractiveness rules. On-brand options:
  - "Pace yourself, it's a long braai."
  - "Water between rounds. No BS."
  - "Zero-alc Margarita for the driver, same real lime."

  For the non-alc SKU, remember that branded alcohol-free products follow the full alcohol code in SA (§1.10.3). Present it as a normal adult choice, never as a "loophole" for under-18s or a way to "keep going".
- **Draft "say this, not that" seeds:**
  - "Get your Fuego fix" → "Your Saturday, poured" / "Real tequila, real lime. Bring a few for the crew."
  - "You need this" → "Made for sharing"
  - "Survived the week? You've earned it" → "Saturday's sorted"
  - "Fuego o'clock" / "It's 5 o'clock somewhere" → "Braai's lit, cans are cold"
  - "Chill out with a Spicy Marg" → "Serve ice-cold, salt rim optional"
  - "Liquid courage for the dance floor" → "Cheers to the ones who bring the playlist"
  - "Packs a 7% punch" → "Real tequila, real lemon & lime"
  - "Tag someone who needs this" → "Tag who's bringing the cooler box"

### Gaps
- No responsible-marketing guide was found that publishes an approved word list such as "bring, share, pour, toast". The bank above is derived from code permissions, not quoted from a guide.
- Diageo's and AB InBev's marketing codes (which reportedly bar "alcohol as a solution to problems") were not retrieved; their wording is **[unverified]**.
- No evaluation was found of whether Heineken's campaigns changed drinking behaviour. Only Heineken's own claims and press coverage were retrieved.
- No SA brand example of humorous "pace yourself" moderation messaging was located within the tool budget.

## 6. Stories and Reels traps: polls and stickers, countdowns, need-a-drink GIFs and memes, "tag someone who needs this", reposting UGC with heavy drinking or under-25s

### Takeaway
Rulings show that Stories/Reels formats get no special leniency. Memes, jokes, hashtags, influencer before-and-after edits and repeated-pour visuals are judged on overall impression. The highest-risk interactive mechanics are volume prompts ("how many cans deep?"), countdowns that cast the week as something to endure, "needs this" tags, and reposting UGC (which, once adopted, becomes the brand's own marketing) that shows multiple drinks or people who look under 25. Industry digital standards (IARD; DF-SA) require a UGC policy, forward-advice notices and responsibility messaging on shareable assets.

### Cited Findings
- **Memes are marketing.** In Drink Doctor (2017) the advertiser said it "had only attached their brand to popular internet memes". The ASA still found the memes implied "alcohol could overcome boredom, loneliness and other personal problems". — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Boredom and loneliness"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-boredom-and-loneliness.html)
- **Humour and parody don't save it.** Blackrose (2019), a parody of "If at first you don't succeed…", was upheld; "humour does not invalidate the need to comply with the Code". Hi-Spirits/Fireball (2013): the "written in jest" defence was rejected. — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Therapeutic claims"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html); [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Boredom and loneliness"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-boredom-and-loneliness.html)
- **Hashtags and captions count.** In La Vieille Ferme (2026) the "#selfcare" hashtag, the caption and even the "Keywords" block "reinforced that impression". — [ASA ruling](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/perrin-et-fils-sas-g26-1334485-perrin-et-fils-sas.html)
- **Visual volume cues.** In La Vieille Ferme, "someone filling their glass repeatedly from three large boxes" enhanced the excessive-drinking finding. In Served, showing "one drink in her hand and no other drinks were visible" helped the "day drinking" post pass. — [ASA La Vieille Ferme](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/perrin-et-fils-sas-g26-1334485-perrin-et-fils-sas.html); [ASA Served Drinks](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/served-drinks-ltd-g22-1146766-served-drinks-ltd.html)
- **Promotions and volume:** CAP: "Marketing communications that include a promotion must not imply, condone or encourage excessive consumption of alcohol" (18.10). CAP 18.1: "they should not encourage excessive drinking". — [CAP s.18](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/18.html)
- **Before/after edits and soundtrack lyrics (Reels/TikTok).** The Muff Liquor Co. video escalated dance energy from non-alcoholic drinks to "Muff and Tonic", captioned "If drinks were dance moves", over lyrics "I'll be fu**ed up if you can't be right here". This was found to imply mood and confidence change, that "sobriety was boring", and to promote excessive consumption. The TikTok version was also ruled as directed at under-18s (CAP 18.15) because of the platform and the influencer's appeal, and the post lacked an "#ad" label. — [RPC summary of ASA ruling, 31 March 2023](https://www.rpclegal.com/snapshots/advertising-and-marketing/spring-2023/asa-slams-social-media-post-for-breaching-rules-on-alcohol-advertising/)
- **Under-25s in content:** CAP: "People under 25 may be shown in marketing communications, for example, in the context of family celebrations, but must be obviously not drinking." — [CAP s.18](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/18.html)
- **Under-21s in SA content:** celebrities and social media personalities must "appear to a reasonable person to be, at least 21 years of age"; "No celebrities may be seen to be consuming alcohol"; and "if they specifically address their audience, their message must include a responsible drinking statement" (§1.1.4). — [DF-SA Code](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct_-marketing-and-communications-2023---arb-version-final-mar-2024.pdf)
- **SA digital audience and UGC rules:**
  - Digital placement only where "at least 70% of the audience is reasonably expected to be of legal drinking age or older" (§1.1.1).
  - Responsible drinking messages "must be used on websites, social media platforms, and shareable, downloadable assets including videos, photos, applications, or user-generated content that is incorporated into alcohol branded Commercial Communications"; on small screens the "Not for Under 18" token may be used (§1.8.2).
  - "Any shareable commercial assets… must include appropriate responsibility messaging" (§3.8.2).
  - Brand pages need a "clearly visible responsibility message… permanently displayed" (§3.8.1) and an age gate (§3.8.4).

  — [DF-SA Code](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct_-marketing-and-communications-2023---arb-version-final-mar-2024.pdf)
- **IARD Digital Guiding Principles (global industry standard),** five safeguards:
  1. "Age-affirmation mechanism"
  2. "Forward advice notice – A clear statement that content shouldn't be forwarded to anyone under the LPA"
  3. "Responsible drinking message"
  4. "Transparency message"
  5. "User-generated-content policy – A clear statement that any user posts that condone irresponsible drinking will be removed."

  IARD reports Accenture found "a 98.2% full compliance rate in 2024". — [IARD, Digital Guiding Principles](https://www.iard.org/actions/digital-guiding-principles)
- **Tagging spreads content beyond the poster.** In young people's alcohol posts, "alcohol posts were more often placed on participants' timelines by others (tagging; 238/439, 54.2%)". The authors recommend making young people aware that tagging others in alcohol posts "may have unintended negative consequences". — [Hendriks et al. 2018](https://www.jmir.org/2018/6/e226/)
- **Weekday countdowns:** Diageo "Wednesday… war on mid-week boredom" (2014) and Jack Daniel's "skip to the good part" (2024) breached the Code. Served's "Mid-week drinking season has arrived", read as festive social events with one drink shown, did not. — [ASA/CAP, "Alcohol: Boredom and loneliness"](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-boredom-and-loneliness.html); [ASA Served Drinks](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/served-drinks-ltd-g22-1146766-served-drinks-ltd.html)
- **Women-targeted poll example:** brands asked women which item they "couldn't you live without?" (lipstick, alcohol, chocolate or phone). The researchers read this as giving alcohol "status as an essential good". — [Atkinson et al., "Pretty in Pink"](https://movendi.ngo/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/1-s2.0-S0955395921004655-main.pdf)

### Inferences
Story/Reels trap list for the one-pager. These apply the rulings above; no ruling on an Instagram poll sticker specifically was found.
- **Poll and quiz stickers:**
  - "How many cans deep are you?", "Rate your hangover", "Survived the week? Yes/No" → excessive-consumption, hangover and coping implications (CAP 18.1/18.10; Reserve; La Vieille Ferme).
  - "Which Fuego matches your mood?" → mood-matching echoes Drake & Morgan's "potion to match their emotion" (upheld).
  - "Could you live without it?" → indispensability (CAP 18.6; Fireball; Pretty in Pink poll).
  - Safe swaps: "Paloma or Spicy Marg for the braai?", "Salt rim: yes or no?", "Beach or stoep this Saturday?"
- **Countdown stickers:** a countdown to a launch, a Checkers stock drop or a specific event (e.g. a beach day) is fine. "Countdown to Friday / the weekend / wine o'clock" frames the week as something to endure (Jack Daniel's; Diageo).
- **GIFs and memes:** avoid any "me needing a drink after work / after the kids / after Monday" format, "this is fine" with a drink, and "therapy vs tequila" jokes. Drink Doctor, Blackrose and La Vieille Ferme show that memes and jokes are assessed as ads.
- **"Tag someone who needs this":** the "need vs enjoy" rule (Portman, CAP). Tagging also pushes content to people the brand hasn't age-screened (Hendriks; IARD forward-advice principle). Swap to "Tag your braai crew (18+)" or "Tag who's on cooler-box duty".
- **Reposting UGC:** once reposted, follower content becomes brand marketing. A pre-repost checklist:
  - Everyone looks clearly over 25 (CAP benchmark; DF-SA minimum 21 for personalities). The SA general model-age rule was not verified (see Gaps).
  - One drink per person visible; no tables of empties or "cans deep" stacks.
  - No drinking in or near the water or before driving.
  - Captions free of coping, need or "fix" words.
  - Add the SA responsibility token ("Not for Persons Under 18") on any incorporated UGC (§1.8.2).
  - Remove comments that condone irresponsible drinking (IARD UGC policy).
- **Influencer and creator Reels:**
  - No before/after "energy" edits (Muff).
  - No lyrics celebrating getting wasted (Muff).
  - Clear "#ad" disclosure (Muff).
  - SA personalities must be 21+, not drinking on camera, and include a responsible drinking statement if they address the audience (§1.1.4).

### Gaps
- No ASA, Portman or ARB ruling was found that turns specifically on an Instagram poll sticker, a countdown sticker or a "tag a friend" mechanic. These are applied by analogy.
- The general South African age rule for people *shown* in alcohol content (as opposed to celebrities) was not confirmed in the DF-SA extract read here, so whether SA uses 18, 21 or 25 for models is **[unverified]** in these notes. Other researchers covering SA codes should confirm.
- No CAP AdviceOnline page specifically on brand liability for reposted UGC was opened. The UGC position rests on IARD's DGP, DF-SA §1.8.2 and general principle, not a quoted ASA statement.
- Meta's and TikTok's own branded-content policies for alcohol were not retrieved (the Muff summary notes TikTok's ban on alcohol advertising).
