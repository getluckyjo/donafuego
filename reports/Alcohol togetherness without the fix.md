# Sell the occasion, never the fix

The brands that sell togetherness well, from Heineken, Guinness and Diageo's portfolio to Corona, Casamigos and Patrón and, at home, Castle Lager and Brutal Fruit, all follow one structural rule: the occasion is already good, the people make it, and the drink is what someone brings and shares. It never lifts a mood, rescues a bad day or becomes something you need. Every code examined writes that rule down. The UK bars implying that anyone "might need (as opposed to merely enjoy)" a drink, and South Africa's Drinks Federation (DF-SA) code bars suggesting that drinking can "solve personal problems" (cl. 2.4.2), has "curative qualities" or works as a "sedative, or tranquiliser" (cl. 2.7.4), or glamourises "drug culture" (cl. 2.8.2). "Get your Fuego fix" engages all three: Cambridge defines a fix as a dose of a drug or substance someone needs, and in 2025 the Advertising Regulatory Board (ARB) ordered a Strongbow ad withdrawn for a gentler version of the same relief story. Doña Fuego's owner is not a DF-SA member, but that gives little cover, because the ARB rules on non-members and refers them to the Minister or Liquor Board, DF-SA files complaints itself, and Meta makes compliance with "established industry codes" a condition of alcohol ads. The bigger exposures in the brand's existing content are structural rather than verbal: the ARB has found that even a closed bottle on a beach towel condones illegal beach drinking, "7%" may appear only as label information (which rules out the headline line in three of the four draft Cooler Box ad sets), and the Non-Alcoholic Margarita, because it carries the alcohol brand name, falls under the full alcohol code and cannot be positioned "for the driver" the way unbranded alcohol-free drinks can. Where the South African texts disagree, the July 2026 DF-SA code is the current and stricter text, and complying with it also satisfies the ARB's February 2026 appendix.

## Winning brands make the people the cause and the drink the guest

**Heineken's repeatable move is to name an enemy of good company and let the people beat it, with the beer present but never decisive.** The enemies it picks are political division ("Worlds Apart", 2017), drink stereotypes ("Cheers to All", 2020), phones ("The Boring Phone", 2024; "Social Off Socials", 2025) and drink-driving ("When You Drive, Never Drink"); they are never boredom, stress or a bad day. Behind the work sits a code that says the company will "NEVER PRESENT OUR BRANDS AS AN ENABLER FOR … A BETTER SOCIAL LIFE" and commits **10% of Heineken media spend** to responsible drinking ([Heineken Responsible Marketing Code](https://heineken.hr/uploaded/OUR%20RESPONSIBLE%20MARKETING%20CODE_UPDATE%202018-1571403932626.pdf)). It sells: Edelman reports a **7.3% rise in UK beer sales in the 12 weeks after Worlds Apart** ([Edelman](https://www.edelman.com/work/heineken-worlds-apart)), and Kantar ranked Cheers to All top for creative effectiveness in 2020, praising it as "not preachy" ([Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/brand-marketing/these-were-the-most-effective-ads-of-2020-according-to-kantar/)). Heineken also shows where the edge lies. LePub's Bruno Bertelli said of The Boring Phone that "it is Heineken that is connecting people" ([LBB](https://lbbonline.com/news/dialling-down-distractions-inside-heinekens-boring-phone-with-bruno-bertelli)), and "The Closer", a bottle opener that puts work laptops to sleep, ties the first beer to the end of the working day ([Marketing Dive](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/heineken-bottle-opener-remote-work-apps/624680/)), the structure the UK regulator found against when Jack Daniel's promised to "skip to the good part". The best films get away with it because the connection happens first and the beer arrives afterwards.

**Guinness and Diageo supply the clearest model lines.** The Guinness "Lovely Day" revival centres on "a group of friends trying to enjoy the sunshine and a pint together" and won the IPA's President's Prize in 2024 ([Creative Salon](https://creative.salon/articles/features/guinness-brand-effectiveness-amv-bbdo)); in Ireland it lifted penetration among 18–34s **from 17.7% in 2019 to 23.5% in 2024** and reversed a ten-year share decline ([The Drum](https://www.thedrum.com/awards-case-study/how-guinness-redefined-summer-with-a-vibrant-transformation-campaign)). Its responsible-drinking work is branded and funny rather than preachy. Guinness Clear relaunched tap water as a product at Six Nations stadiums to make ordering water "an active and positive choice" ([LBB](https://lbbonline.com/news/amv-bbdo-and-guinness-rebrand-tap-water-to-guinness-clear)), and the St Patrick's Day 0.0 push ("Buy 1, get 0.0 free") lifted Irish supermarket sales of Guinness 0.0 by **50%** ([Marketing Week](https://www.marketingweek.com/guinness-alcohol-free-st-patricks-day/)). Diageo's 2023 marketing code then states the governing principle better than any regulator does: brands "may be portrayed as the perfect choice for already energetic or relaxing occasions, but we will not imply that they are the catalyst for any change in energy, state of alertness or states of mental or physical relaxation" (§06h), and the company will never imply "it is wrong or foolish to refuse a drink, even in a humorous manner" (§02) ([Diageo Marketing Code 2023](https://www.diageo.com/~/media/Files/D/Diageo-V2/Diageo-Corp/our-business/corporate-governance/code-of-business-conduct/policies-and-standards/diageo-marketing-code-2023.pdf)).

**The brands closest to Doña Fuego's world each own a place and a small ritual, and make product truth the emotional hero.** Corona sells the beach and "the ritual of adding a slice of lime", not a feeling the beer produces ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260604412899/en/Corona-Launches-2026-Beach-100-Guide-Invites-The-World-To-Explore-The-Outdoors-This-Summer)), and Aperol's 2025 campaign rests on "our colour, our perfect serve, our sociability" ([Campari Group](https://www.camparigroup.com/en/news/2025-05-13/aperol-launches-global-campaign-kickstart-summer-2025)). Tequila brands that escape the shots image do it through friendship and the margarita. Casamigos's headline is "For Friends. By Friends." ([Casamigos](https://www.casamigos.com/en-us/our-story)), and Patrón's 2026 transparency campaign rests on being "made with just three ingredients and nothing else", backed by a brand survey in which **88%** said they would consider switching if their tequila held undisclosed additives ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/patron-tequila-continues-its-transparency-campaign-asking-drinkers-whats-actually-in-their-tequila-302848950.html)). Cutwater's "Open the Bar" sold canned cocktails as a way of "bringing the best of the bar where they are", the beach and home entertaining included ([Anheuser-Busch](https://www.anheuser-busch.com/newsroom/open-the-bar-cutwater-campaign)), which is "share of cooler box" in an American accent. The cautionary tequila case is Jose Cuervo's 2026 "Summer Fridays", which urged margarita lovers "to shut their laptops and start their weekends early" ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/416628/cuervo-tequila-poplife-promote-summer-fridays.html)), a reward-for-work frame. Smirnoff's "WE DO WE" celebrates "the power of the collective" ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/smirnoff-champions-the-power-of-the-collective-in-new-global-brand-positioning-we-do-we-301851074.html)), but the press framed it as tackling "the loneliness epidemic" ([Ad Age](https://adage.com/article/marketing-news-strategy/how-smirnoff-addressing-loneliness-epidemic-new-campaign/2531691/)), and loneliness is a word the UK code names outright.

**South African leaders anchor togetherness in national rituals and causes.** Castle Lager framed its Heritage Day 2025 "World's Biggest Braai" as "uniting thousands across the country in a shared moment of national pride" ([SA Rugby Mag](https://www.sarugbymag.co.za/castle-lager-big-save-invite-sa-worlds-biggest-braai/)), and News24 reproduced a July 2026 #ad Castle post by the influencer Jana Swart as acceptable practice; it pairs national pride, a participation prompt ("comment with your best Braai Hacks") and vernacular ("Ons gaan nou braai!") ([News24](https://www.news24.com/business/companies/ad-regulator-blasts-sab-in-landmark-influencer-ruling-20260728-1113)). Brutal Fruit's Effie-winning SheBeen, a women-only bar, and its 2024 "Phones Down, Glasses Up" Women's Day picnic make sisterhood the subject ([Bizcommunity](https://www.bizcommunity.com/article/brutal-fruit-shebeen-campaign-empowers-women-in-a-way-that-is-true-to-the-brand-142686a); [The Mercury](https://themercury.co.za/lifestyle/partnered/2024-08-15-womens-day-unplugged-brutal-fruit-spritzers-phone-free-picnic-sparks-real-connections/)). Carling Black Label's #NoExcuse went further and named alcohol's harms in the brand's own work: "Soccer Song for Change" (2018) used a derby crowd to call out the abuse that spikes after big matches, "where tension is high and much alcohol is consumed" ([WPP](https://www.wpp.com/featured/work/ogilvy---carling-black-label-soccer-song-for-change)). Savanna provides the template for unwanted user content. When #SavannaChallenge drinking videos trended in 2021, the brand said the challenge was "in no way initiated, supported or promoted by the Savanna brand" and invited people to tag "responsible and safe memorable moments" instead ([TimesLIVE](https://www.timeslive.co.za/news/south-africa/2021-01-06-savanna-distances-itself-from-irresponsible-and-damaging-viral-trend/)). The local counter-example is Bernini's 2024 claim that its taste "turns ordinary moments into extraordinary memories" ([Bizcommunity](https://bizcommunity.ug/article/berninis-audacity-to-be-campaign-encourages-women-to-make-the-first-move-128906a)), structurally the claim the UK regulator upheld a complaint against when Freixenet said any moment is "made just that bit more fun" with its bubbles ([ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/freixenet-uk-ltd-a15-316701.html)). Doña Fuego's nearest shelf rival, Spearhead's Patch, already sells a Margarita and a Paloma in more than 100 Checkers stores ([Food Business MEA](https://www.foodbusinessmea.com/spearhead-spirits-launches-south-africas-first-bar-strength-rtd-cocktails/)).

**The research gives a reason beyond compliance to stay in the social register.** The field's key review found that "social motives appear to be associated with moderate alcohol use, enhancement with heavy drinking, and coping motives with alcohol-related problems" ([Kuntsche et al. 2005](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272735805000759)). A meta-analysis of **229 studies covering 130,705 people** confirmed coping and enhancement motives as the strongest predictors of drinking problems ([Bresin & Mekawi 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:33206387&resultType=core&format=json)), and among 22,841 young European drinkers aged 13 to 16, those who drank to cope suffered more consequences "over and above their alcohol use" ([Wicki et al. 2017](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:28580680&resultType=core&format=json)). Women interviewed in Delft township venues described the frame that "fix" copy reproduces: "I need to drink to calm me down" and "When the weekend comes and I drink my alcohol, I don't think about [my problems] anymore" ([Choi et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4162844/)). Social content also performs. In one study 97% of young adults' alcohol posts showed a positive social context, and posts with people in them averaged **35.5 likes against 10.3** for posts without people ([Hendriks et al. 2018](https://www.jmir.org/2018/6/e226/)). "Fix", "need" and "survive the week" speak to the coping motive; "bring a few for the braai" speaks to the social one. Social motives still link to harm through volume, so social copy needs volume discipline too. One limit applies to all of this: the researchers could not read brand Instagram accounts directly, so the brand evidence comes from campaign press and case studies rather than caption-by-caption analysis.

## Every code draws one line: enjoy, never need

**The UK codes are the strictest mature benchmark, and they say "need" out loud.** CAP rule 18.3 allows drinking to be "portrayed as sociable or thirst-quenching" but not as "a key component of the success of a personal relationship or social event"; 18.6 bars implying alcohol is "indispensable" or can "overcome boredom, loneliness or other problems"; 18.7 bars "therapeutic qualities" and portraying alcohol "as capable of changing mood"; and 18.2 bars claiming it enhances "confidence or popularity" ([CAP Code s.18](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/18.html)). CAP's guidance adds the sentence that settles the Fuego question: marketers must not "imply that anyone might need (as opposed to merely enjoy) an alcoholic drink". Featuring people "who are clearly stressed or more anxious than normal" is likely to breach the code, and "chill with your mates" may pass, but not if the drink makes people "chilled" ([CAP: Therapeutic claims](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)). On parties the guidance is just as direct: "If an otherwise dull party becomes a social triumph as a result of alcohol, the ad will almost certainly breach the Code" ([CAP: Social events](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-social-events.html)). The Portman Group, which covers packaging and promotions, is blunter still. "Do not claim that anyone needs, as opposed to likes, or enjoys, a drink"; "Alcohol must not be the catalyst for change of mood or mental state"; relaxation is acceptable only when "attributed to one's surroundings/setting, and not the alcohol"; and producers are encouraged to build emotive copy around "taste and quality" ([Portman 3.2(j) guidance](https://www.portmangroup.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/guidance-on-rule-3-2j-therapeutic-qualities.pdf)). Its social-success guidance names "Icebreaker", "Social Lubricator" and "Dutch Courage" as unacceptable ([Portman 3.2(e) guidance](https://www.portmangroup.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/06/Guidance-Note-Rule-3.2e-Social-success-or-popularity.pdf)), and in 2023 a single back-label claim that sage and lemon would "cure a sour mood" was enough to uphold a complaint against Engine Organic Gin ([Portman Group](https://www.portmangroup.org.uk/complaint-against-engine-organic-gin-upheld-for-suggestion-of-therapeutic-qualities/)).

**UK rulings turn on one question: what state are people in before the drink, and what changes because of it?** Upheld cases cast the drink as the turning point; cleared cases show people who were already happy.

Humour, memes, parody and influencer delivery have not saved a post; CAP states that "humour does not invalidate the need to comply with the Code" ([CAP: Therapeutic claims](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-therapeutic-claims.html)). The researchers found no ruling on the word "fix" itself, so applying these rulings to it is reasoning by analogy, but the analogues ("emotional support", "erase today", "survival kit", "got your back") are close.

**American, European and global industry codes repeat the line in weaker or more procedural form.** DISCUS bars "curative or therapeutic claims" and claims that drinking brings "social, professional, educational, or athletic success or status" ([DISCUS Code 2025](https://distilledspirits.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Final-2025-DISCUS-Code.pdf)), while the Beer Institute allows beer "as a part of personal and social interactions" and only bars claiming that people "cannot solve social, personal, or physical problems without beer" ([Beer Institute Code 2026](https://www.beerinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/260105_BeerInstitute_Memo_Static_AdGuidelines_Compressed.pdf)), a narrower test than the UK's. What the US codes offer instead is digital mechanics: both set a **73.8%** adult-audience threshold (the January 2026 Beer Institute text replaces the 73.6% that some secondary sources still quote), both require models aged 25 or over, and DISCUS wants user content monitored "each business day when possible, but, at a minimum, every five business days". The EU directive bars TV alcohol advertising from presenting alcohol as "a stimulant, a sedative or a means of resolving personal conflicts" (Art. 22(d)) ([AVMSD](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02010L0013-20181218)). For producers' own channels, the IARD's five digital safeguards are the global hygiene standard: an age-affirmation mechanism, a forward advice notice, a responsible drinking message, a transparency message and a policy of removing user posts that condone irresponsible drinking ([IARD](https://www.iard.org/actions/digital-guiding-principles)). Its influencer principles ask for influencers aged 25 or over where platform age-gating is not effective and give **72 hours** to fix or remove a post ([IARD Influencer Guiding Principles](https://www.iard.org/getmedia/fa30bdb7-0567-4da8-87b1-50abc47fc1ac/14092021-Influencer-Guiding-Principles-FINAL_1.pdf)); South Africa's current code cuts that window to 24.

**Platforms police the audience rather than the message, with one exception.** Meta requires 18+ targeting for alcohol ads in South Africa and has no content rule of its own on mood or coping; instead it requires ads to comply with "required or established industry codes" ([Meta Ad Standards: Alcohol](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/alcohol)), and branded content must be age-gated to 18+ ([Meta Branded Content Policies](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/221149188908254)). Nothing in Meta's own wording would catch "fix", but a breach of the code is also a breach of Meta's policy. TikTok allows South African alcohol ads only through a TikTok sales representative, with registration documents, disclaimers and an 18+ restriction, and does not allow ads for bars or "alcohol delivery services" ([TikTok: market-specific requirements](https://ads.tiktok.com/resources/help/article/alcohol-market-specific-requirements)). Its global policy bars featuring anyone under 25 and "offering alcohol as a prize or reward", and requires advertisers to "clearly state the alcoholic content and strength of a beverage" ([TikTok Ads Policy: Alcohol](https://ads.tiktok.com/resources/help/article/tiktok-ads-policy-alcohol)), which collides with South Africa's ban on featuring strength. One source in the notes described TikTok as banning alcohol ads outright; that description predates mid-2024, and the market page updated in September 2026 is current. TikTok's branded content policy also treats "alcohol-free or no-alcohol alternatives" from alcohol brands as restricted content, limited to viewers aged 18+ in South Africa ([TikTok Branded Content Policy](https://www.tiktok.com/legal/page/global/bc-policy/en)). Google is the exception that writes content rules: ads may not "imply that alcohol is necessary for a successful or healthy lifestyle" ([Google Ads: Irresponsible alcohol advertising](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/17514803?hl=en)), which makes need-framing a line drawn independently by the UK regulator, the Portman Group and a platform. Age targeting alone does not excuse youth appeal: the ASA upheld a 2025 Bargain Booze Santa video despite its 18+ targeting, noting that Facebook and Instagram "do not enforce robust age-verification systems" ([The Drinks Business](https://www.thedrinksbusiness.com/2025/05/alcohol-brands-under-fire-for-irresponsible-social-media-ads/)).

## South Africa's code bans the fix in three clauses and reaches non-members

**The ARA code no longer exists. The DF-SA code replaced it, and it reaches brands that never signed it.** The ARA's code gave way to Aware.org's in January 2020 ([Adams & Adams](https://www.adams.africa/jenny-pienaar/developments-in-alcohol-advertising-how-we-market-today-will-determine-if-we-can-market-tomorrow/)); DF-SA adapted that code in its August 2023 edition and took over oversight of commercial communication, and the newest text, "Code-of-Conduct-2026.pdf", was posted in July 2026. It covers "digital and social media" and demands adherence to "both the letter and spirit" ([DF-SA Code 2026](https://www.drinksfederationsa.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Code-of-Conduct-2026.pdf)). Formally it binds only DF-SA and Aware.org members, a list that runs to DGB, Diageo, Halewood, Heineken, Pernod Ricard, RGBC and SAB ([DF-SA](https://www.drinksfederationsa.co.za/home/)); Doña Distillery, Doña Fuego's owner, is not on it. That gives thin cover. For a business that has not signed, the ARB "shall nonetheless render a decision", recommend a sanction and refer the matter to "the appropriate Minister, Liquor Board, and/or the Department" (cl. 3.3.6.3). The Constitutional Court's 2023 Bliss Brands judgment held that a non-member who answers a complaint on the merits submits to the ARB, and that where one does not, a ruling can still direct ARB members to "refuse to accept a non-member's advertisement" ([Constitutional Court](https://www.concourt.org.za/index.php/judgement/516-advertising-regulatory-board-npc-and-others-v-bliss-brands-pty-limited-cct132-22)). The notes describe the 2022 Supreme Court of Appeal step differently, but the Constitutional Court judgment is the controlling text and both accounts agree on this practical position. DF-SA also files complaints itself: it brought the 2024 case against Don Billiato, a non-member tequila brand, and ARB chief executive Gail Schimmel called it "the industry itself saying we are not prepared to put up with this type of advertising" ([News24](https://www.news24.com/business/companies/very-much-a-party-vibe-arb-orders-cassper-nyovests-tequila-brand-to-pull-ad-glamourising-drinking-20240718)). Sanctions run from "a written warning for a first offence" to a ban on all marketing for up to three months for the worst repeat offenders, and they weigh whether the business "sought pre-clearance advice that it subsequently followed" ([DF-SA Code 2026](https://www.drinksfederationsa.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Code-of-Conduct-2026.pdf)). The ARB's pre-clearance service is optional and non-binding and is offered to signatories and to businesses whose licence is conditioned on the code; whether a non-member can use it is unconfirmed.

**Three clauses cover the fix, and two more spell out what is allowed.** No South African text names "fix", "need", "self-care", "earned it" or "therapy"; the code works by principle. Drinking may not be shown to "solve personal problems, overcome inhibitions, shyness, or social barriers, or enable social acceptance", or to cause "business, social, professional … or other personal success" (cl. 2.4.2); alcohol may not be offered as having "curative qualities" or as a "performance enhancer, stimulant, sedative, or tranquiliser" (cl. 2.7.4); and communications may not "portray or in any way endorse or glamourise illicit drugs, drug culture" (cl. 2.8.2) ([DF-SA Code 2026](https://www.drinksfederationsa.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Code-of-Conduct-2026.pdf)). On the permission side, depicting "responsible drinking among adults as a relaxed, sociable, and enjoyable part of a well-lived life" has "a role to play" (cl. 2.2.1), and communications "may reflect South Africa's diversity of people from all walks of life enjoying various everyday social experiences and occasions" (cl. 2.4.1). A braai with friends falls squarely inside the permission; a can that rescues a bad day falls squarely outside it.

**Recent ARB rulings map almost one-to-one onto a social manager's risk list.** The closest precedent to the Fuego story is **Strongbow Gold Apple (June 2025)**: a stressed office worker, a paper aeroplane that turns into a bottle, feet on the desk and monitors reading 17:00. The advertiser called it a metaphor for "leaving work behind"; the ARB found viewers would "reasonably" read it "as portraying alcohol consumption as a solution to workplace stress", because it "directly ties emotional relief and relaxation to the product", and ordered it withdrawn ([News24](https://www.news24.com/business/consumer-lookout/strongbow-ad-promotes-booze-to-help-manage-work-stress-watchdog-finds-20250605-0715)). **Don Billiato (2024)**, Cassper Nyovest's tequila, lost over "enjoy a taste of wealth", and its "tongue in cheek" defence failed ([News24](https://www.news24.com/business/companies/very-much-a-party-vibe-arb-orders-cassper-nyovests-tequila-brand-to-pull-ad-glamourising-drinking-20240718)). **Wildebeest Brandewyn (May 2026)** shows the limit: "Brandy is like love – the more you get, the happier you are" was cleared as comic and nostalgic, though the ARB recorded "significant discomfort" and restated that positive associations are allowed "provided it does not explicitly promise success or claim to solve personal problems" ([Bizcommunity](https://www.bizcommunity.com/article/arb-clears-wildebeest-brandewyn-ad-accused-of-promoting-binge-drinking-067655a)). **Brutal Fruit (February 2025)** matters most for Doña Fuego's occasions. SAB argued that the bottle on the beach towel was closed; the ARB called that "disingenuous", because a consumer "will most likely believe that all the settings are places the drink can be consumed", and concluded the ad "condones and in fact encourages bringing alcohol to the beach and consuming it on the beach. This is illegal." ([Bizcommunity](https://www.bizcommunity.com/article/arb-ruling-should-alcohol-brands-advertise-on-the-beach-365388a)). **Castle Lager (July 2026)** went against SAB because an influencer's branded post on her "personal" X account carried no #ad, while the ARB accepted the "18+ SAB SHARP responsibility icon" as a small-screen responsibility mark ([News24](https://www.news24.com/business/companies/ad-regulator-blasts-sab-in-landmark-influencer-ruling-20260728-1113)). And two 2026 rulings against RTD and seltzer calorie claims, Solstice's "27 calories per can" and South Side Hard Soda's 72 calories, show that RTD health-adjacent copy is now being tested ([Bizcommunity](https://www.bizcommunity.com/article/this-alcohol-brands-low-calorie-claim-didnt-hold-up-rules-arb-392298a); [News24](https://www.news24.com/business/companies/sa-spirit-cooler-slammed-for-misleading-low-calorie-claim-20260717-0970)).

**Statute is thin, but the politics are not.** The Liquor Act bars advertising liquor "in a false or misleading manner" or "in a manner intended to target or attract minors" (s 9(1)(a)), and supplying liquor to minors (s 10(1)) ([SAFLII](https://www.saflii.org/za/legis/consol_act/la2003107/)). The Western Cape Liquor Act has no rules on advertising content, but it makes it an offence to drink in a vehicle on a public road, including a parked one, and bans drinking at service stations (ss 80(1), 81(1)) ([Western Cape Liquor Act](https://www.sawis.co.za/winelaw/download/Western_Cape_Liquor_Act,_as_amended.pdf)). The seven rotating health messages, including "Alcohol is addictive", are label requirements rather than social-post requirements ([Acts Online](https://www.acts.co.za/foodstuffs-cosmetics-and-disinfectants-act-1972/r764_annexure_a__health_messag)). The pending threat is the EFF's Liquor Amendment Bill B21-2025, which would prohibit "the advertising, promotion or product placement of liquor, in all forms of media" and the use of any brand element; it was introduced on 8 September 2025, and Parliament still lists it at the introduction stage ([Parliament](https://www.parliament.gov.za/bill/2325059)). It is not law, but it is the backdrop to every careless post. Schimmel's own warning was that "if the government believes that the alcohol industry is behaving in an irresponsible way, they are going to ban the advertising of alcohol" ([News24](https://www.news24.com/business/companies/very-much-a-party-vibe-arb-orders-cassper-nyovests-tequila-brand-to-pull-ad-glamourising-drinking-20240718)).

### Two texts, two numbering systems: follow the 2026 DF-SA code

**The notes quote three versions of the same code, and the numbers differ because the 2026 text inserts new clauses.** The ARB's Appendix A of 25 March 2024 ([ARB](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/lappendix-a_alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct-mar-2024.pdf)) and its Alcohol Appendix v2026-02, headed "(2025)" and listed on the ARB's Codes page ([ARB Appendix Alcohol v2026-02](https://arb.org.za/code-file-downloads/Appendix_Alcohol_v2026-02.pdf); [ARB, The Codes](https://www.arb.org.za/The_Codes/)), number the standards 1.1 to 1.11. The DF-SA's July 2026 PDF numbers them from 2.1 and adds new material: cl. 2.4.1 (everyday social occasions), cl. 2.1.10 (frequent moderation of user content) and a new section 2.5 on paid influencers, reposting and 24-hour correction, which pushes every later section up by one. The core content wording is the same in every version, so the analysis of "fix" does not change; the versions differ on the new digital clauses, the minimum age for paid influencers, the wording of the responsibility token and the alcohol-free definitions. **The DF-SA 2026 text is the current one.** It is the newest text from the body that owns the code, and it is a stricter superset, so complying with it also satisfies the ARB appendix. Two cautions apply: the 2026 PDF's own preamble still calls it the "August 2023 edition" and no adoption date was found, and the ARB cites its own numbering (Strongbow was decided under cl. 1.4.1), so any response to a complaint should cite both. Where the wording differs, use the DF-SA wording, which meets both texts.

### Age rules run at 18, 21 or 25 depending on who is on screen

**The notes disagree on age because they read different texts.** South Africa's legal drinking age is 18 (Liquor Act s 10; Western Cape Act s 54). The current DF-SA text sets no general minimum age for models in brand-produced content that the researchers could find. It sets 21 for celebrities, a term that covers social media personalities "paid or unpaid", none of whom may "be seen to be consuming alcohol" (cl. 2.1.6); 21 for promoters (cl. 2.3.3); an apparent age of 25 for paid influencers, who also need an audience at least 70% confirmed adult (cl. 2.5.3); and over 18 for anyone clearly visible in user content on a brand page (cl. 2.1.9) ([DF-SA Code 2026](https://www.drinksfederationsa.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Code-of-Conduct-2026.pdf)). The ARB appendix has no influencer section, so it applies 21 to celebrities and influencers alike ([ARB Appendix A](https://www.arb.org.za/assets/lappendix-a_alcohol-industry-code-of-conduct-mar-2024.pdf)). The 25 rule that some notes treat as South African comes from Aware.org's 2020 code, under which nobody "depicted in the act of drinking" could be, or look, younger than 25 ([Aware.org toolkit](https://assets-global.website-files.com/64afbf0da65d7b3be0358d54/64c9132703ce31f755a5ee3a_Code-Toolkit-1.pdf)). Internationally, 25 is the norm: CAP 18.16, Portman 3.2(i), the DISCUS and Beer Institute model rules, and TikTok's ban on featuring anyone under 25 in alcohol ads ([CAP](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/18.html); [TikTok](https://ads.tiktok.com/resources/help/article/tiktok-ads-policy-alcohol)). Doña Fuego's own shoot brief already rejects any frame in which someone holding a can "could read as under 25", which is the right house rule.

## What "get your Fuego fix" and the existing feed must change

### The fix story fails on meaning, not on a technicality

**"Get your Fuego fix" fails four tests at once.** Its ordinary meaning is dependence: Cambridge defines the noun as "an amount of an illegal drug, or of another substance that has an effect on someone", with the example "He was shaking badly and needed a fix" ([Cambridge Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/fix)), which engages the drug-culture bar (cl. 2.8.2) and the "curative … sedative, or tranquiliser" bar (cl. 2.7.4). Because a fix answers a lack, it also engages the "solve personal problems" bar (cl. 2.4.2) that sank Strongbow. It frames need rather than enjoyment, exactly the "need (as opposed to merely enjoy)" framing CAP and Portman name. And it addresses one person and a craving, with no people, food or occasion, which is the coping register rather than the social one. Sixty60's on-demand delivery sharpens the craving-then-relief reading, and the line promotes a product whose label must carry warnings such as "Alcohol is addictive". The researchers disagree on severity. The South African analysis calls it a moderate-risk breach of the code's spirit rather than a clear breach of its letter, because no clause names the word and Wildebeest shows the ARB gives humour some room; the international analysis rates it high-risk under every benchmark. Both are right about different things. A single story would probably not, on its own, produce an adverse ARB ruling, but it has no defence if challenged: Wildebeest survived because viewers were unlikely to take the line literally, and with "fix" the literal meaning is the problem. The realistic costs are a complaint, a screenshot in a live debate about banning alcohol ads, and a precedent inside the team.

**The repair is quick and quiet.** A story leaves the feed after 24 hours, but highlights, the archive, screenshots and reshares remain, so remove it from highlights and any brand reposts, ask anyone who reshared it to take it down, and record the date. The code's only stated correction window is 24 hours from notification (cl. 2.5.8), and sanctions weigh how a business behaved, so a documented takedown is the best mitigation. No public statement is needed unless someone complains. If a complaint arrives, take advice before answering on the merits: under Bliss Brands, doing so accepts ARB jurisdiction, while declining still leaves an adverse ruling, a referral and Meta's code condition in play. The lasting fix is procedural. The code expects "appropriate content approval processes prior to publication" (cl. 2.5.7.2), which for a small team means a second person signing off every story.

### The beach is a bigger exposure than the story

**One of Doña Fuego's three occasions collides with a 2025 ruling.** Under Brutal Fruit, a closed product on a beach towel is enough, because viewers read every setting shown as a place to drink, and the ARB treats drinking on the beach as illegal; a cooler box on the sand is the Brutal Fruit pattern exactly. The UK's Portman Group accepts beach scenes with care ([Portman summertime guide](https://www.portmangroup.org.uk/a-summertime-guide-to-responsible-alcohol-marketing/)), but the South African ruling governs South African content. Keep the beach as scenery without product, and put the product on the stoep of the holiday house, at the braai after the beach, in a garden or on a private deck, the way Corona localised its beach positioning in South Africa through sunsets and coastal place names rather than drinking on the sand ([Bizcommunity](https://www.bizcommunity.ug/article/corona-flips-the-script-with-hidden-sunsets-ooh-campaign-471419a)). The 12 September Fresnaye braai already shows the compliant version: `BERN_138`, the sunset toast with the Atlantic behind, and `344294_000012`, the can against Sea Point, were both shot on private property with the sea as backdrop. The same logic keeps cans out of the water, off boats, out of cars (including cars parked on a public road), away from service stations, and out of players' hands during beach cricket or touch rugby (cl. 2.2.3); one global note suggested a Saturday-afternoon game of cricket as a frame, and the South African sport clause rules out cans in players' hands. The Fresnaye selects need a second look on the same basis. `344294_000030` shows a Spicy Margarita floating on the pool and `344294_000022` shows cans on the pool edge. Portman's test is whether an image suggests the drink "has been consumed before or during taking part in water sports or swimming"; the South African code has no swimming clause, but its sobriety clause and the drowning concern in the Brutal Fruit complaint point the same way. Keep the floating can off the feed, use pool-edge frames only when nobody is in the water, and reconsider the next-shoot plan for "two underwater cameras" if their job is product.

### Braai, cooler box and Saturday afternoon are safe if the set-up is

**The braai and Saturday afternoon are safe ground, and the cooler box is safe as long as it means sharing.** Both occasions are what cl. 2.4.1 describes. The risk is in the set-up: "made it to Saturday", "after the week we've had", a 17:00 clock or a Friday countdown turns the weekend into an escape, which is the frame behind the Strongbow ruling and the Delft interviews. "Day drinking" passed the ASA in one relaxed, one-drink scene ([ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/served-drinks-ltd-g22-1146766-served-drinks-ltd.html)), but "Saturday afternoon" or "long lunch" says the same thing without the baggage. "Share of cooler box" is a strategy phrase, not a caption; on screen, the cooler box should be about who is bringing what, never how much. Stacks of empties, "cans deep", "another round" and fill-it-up copy invite the volume reading that drew the ARB's discomfort over Wildebeest, and they breach cl. 2.3.1. Two existing items follow. `BERN_100`, a woman laughing "with an armful of cans clutched to her chest", reads as one person's haul and belongs in feed or texture rather than hero, and the brief's frame 12, "the end of it — empties", should stay unshot or keep the empties incidental to the plates and melted ice.

### "7%" belongs on the can, never in the caption

**The answer is no: "7%" may not appear in captions.** The DF-SA code says labels state alcohol content "in a factual manner only and not as the principal subject", that "non-label forms of Commercial Communications should not feature or depict a product's alcohol content unless the alcohol content by volume is at or below 2.5%", and that "advertising of alcohol content is not permitted" (cl. 2.6.1–2.6.3; ARB cl. 1.5.1–1.5.3) ([DF-SA Code 2026](https://www.drinksfederationsa.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Code-of-Conduct-2026.pdf)). At 7%, Doña Fuego is well above that line, so "7%" stays out of captions, stickers, graphics, hashtags and voiceovers. A pack shot in which the label's ABV is visible in its normal place falls under the label exception; a close-up that makes the number the subject does not. International rules agree: CAP 18.9 says the strength of a relatively strong product "should not be given undue emphasis", CAP's guidance flags "warnings about the product's strength", and Google bars ads that "unduly emphasize high alcohol strength" ([CAP](https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/18.html); [CAP: Challenges](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/alcohol-challenges-bravery-and-machismo.html); [Google Ads](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/6012382?hl=en)). Words that do the same job, such as "strong", "punch", "proper hit" or "fire in a can", fall with the number, and on the Spicy Margarita "heat" should be tied explicitly to the spice. This has a concrete cost. Three of the four ad sets on the Cooler Box go-live sign-off page lead with a 7% line ("Real tequila, real lime, 7%.", "Real jalapeño tequila, lemon and lime, 7%." and "Real tequila, real grapefruit, 7%."); each should lose the number and keep the ingredients, which are the stronger and fully compliant half of the line. The Non-Alc Margarita is the mirror image: at or below 2.5%, its alcohol content may be stated, so its measured ABV can appear, provided it is never set against the 7%. The one platform conflict is TikTok, whose global ad policy requires advertisers to state "alcoholic content and strength"; agree the wording with the TikTok sales representative and with counsel before any paid TikTok campaign, with a small factual line on the end card as the likely compromise.

### The Non-Alc Margarita is an alcohol-brand product in every respect

**Because it carries the Doña Fuego name, the Non-Alcoholic Margarita is treated as alcohol in every respect.** The code exempts alcohol-free drinks only when they "do not share a brand name with an alcohol product" (cl. 1.4). Branded alcohol-free and non-alcohol drinks "may only be promoted and sold to people of legal drinking age" (cl. 2.11.2) and must meet "all requirements of this Code" (cl. 2.11.4), and the freedom to promote alcohol-free drinks for occasions "such as when expecting to drive, attending a business lunch", or "as a sociable option for avoiding over-consuming alcohol", applies only "provided that the beverages do not carry an alcohol brand name" (cl. 2.11.5) ([DF-SA Code 2026](https://www.drinksfederationsa.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Code-of-Conduct-2026.pdf)). So the Non-Alc Margarita gets the 18+ gate, the token, the casting rules and the ban on health claims ("better for you" is out under cl. 2.7.3), and it does not get a designated-driver or pacing line. One research note proposed "Zero-alc Margarita for the driver, same real lime"; the strict text rules that out. SAB's "pacer" positioning for its branded Castle Free ([The Citizen](https://citizen.co.za/lifestyle/food-and-drink/1708368/castle-free-is-sas-first-locally-brewed-alcohol-free-beer/)) shows that the industry reads the clause more loosely, a tension worth putting to counsel before any driver or pacing copy runs. The platforms point the same way: TikTok treats alcohol-free alternatives from alcohol brands as restricted content, and Meta's lighter regime for non-alcoholic ads applies only where the ads "do not depict alcohol or consumption" ([Meta](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/alcohol)), which a feed under a shared brand cannot guarantee. The global model that fits is Heineken's "0.0 Reasons Needed", which refuses to supply a reason at all ("Dieting? Driving? Working late? Wrong.") ([Heineken](https://www.heineken.com/global/en/campaigns/zero-reasons); [Adweek](https://www.adweek.com/creativity/in-heineken-00s-new-ads-it-doesnt-matter-why-youre-going-dry/)). Present the fourth flavour as a choice that needs no excuse, in the same frame as the others, and never as the dull option or the punchline (cl. 2.2.5). Heineken's earlier "Now You Can" put 0.0 at work, at a petrol station and in a car park ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/330394/now-you-can-ads-intro-no-alcohol-heineken-00-in.html?edition=112434)), exactly the occasions the South African clause reserves for unbranded drinks. Both texts' alcohol-free bands cover everything up to 0.5%, so which band applies does not change the treatment; confirm the measured ABV and use the term that matches it. The non-alc was not part of the Fresnaye shoot or the three-flavour Cooler Box ad sets, so these rules apply from its first appearance.

### "No BS", Checkers and Sixty60 prompts, creators, reposts and giveaways

**"No BS" is an asset precisely because it is an ingredient claim.** "Made with real tequila, lemon & lime juice" is the taste-and-quality message Portman encourages, and Patrón built a 2026 campaign on the same idea. The line must stay literally true: the Liquor Act bars "false or misleading" advertising, and the ARB's social media appendix requires messaging that is "responsible and accurate" ([ARB Appendix K](https://arb.org.za/code-file-downloads/Appendix_Social_Media_v2026-02.pdf)). That puts the Paloma line "Grapefruit, tequila, ice. That's the whole recipe." on notice, since it holds only if the can contains nothing else, and the brand's own line names lemon and lime juice. Nor may the claim stretch into "guilt-free", "clean", "light" or calorie claims: "healthier", "better for you" and "safer" are banned outright (cl. 2.7.3), and two RTD calorie claims fell in 2026 for lack of substantiation. Keep the signature phrase attached to the ingredient promise rather than spending it on jokes or moderation lines.

**Retail and delivery prompts are fine with an 18+ audience, with one TikTok exception.** Meta counts "availability of alcohol sales" as promoting alcohol, so "find it at Checkers" posts and ads run 18+, as the draft ad sets already do. TikTok's South African rules bar ads for "alcohol delivery services", so any TikTok ad with "order on Sixty60" as its call to action needs clearing with the TikTok representative first. On any platform, never pair delivery with urgency or need ("need it now?"), which recreates the fix. "Ordered at 4. In the cooler box by 5." (ad set 4) works as a convenience line on a braai image, but it should never sit on workday imagery, where its 17:00 would echo Strongbow. "The drink you bring when everyone else brings wine" and "Three flavours, one cooler box" are model lines, because they are about bringing and choosing.

**The responsibility marks need one standard wording.** The draft ad captions end "Not for sale to persons under the age of 18. Drink responsibly.", wording found in none of the current texts, and the opening film signs off "NOT FOR PERSONS UNDER 18", which is the ARB form. Standardise on the DF-SA token, "Not for Persons Under the Age of 18", and burn it into every image, story frame and video rather than leaving it only in the caption, because the code requires the message on "shareable, downloadable assets" (cl. 2.9.2, 7.8.2).

**Creators, reposts and giveaways carry most of the procedural rules.** Paid creators must look 25 or over, have an audience at least 70% confirmed adult, sign a contract with an alcohol responsibility clause and a disclosure requirement, use age-restriction tools, and be monitored (cl. 2.5.3–2.5.6). After the Castle ruling, #ad goes on every account a paid creator uses, "personal" accounts included, and read with cl. 2.1.6, a creator can hold and recommend the can but should not be seen drinking it. Every repost becomes brand content, so before reposting check that everyone clearly visible looks over 18 (ideally over 25), that there is one drink per person, that no drinking games, water or cars appear, and that the caption is clean; then add the token, and correct anything flagged within 24 hours (cl. 2.1.9, 2.5.7–2.5.8). Giveaways must state that under-18s cannot enter (cl. 2.3.2) and must not reward volume (cl. 2.3.1). The Consumer Protection Act adds no entry fee or price premium (electronic entry costs are capped at R1.50), written rules prepared in advance, the six items listed in s 36(5) stated in the offer, no prizes to staff, suppliers or their families, an independent accountant, auditor, attorney or advocate to certify the draw, records kept for three years, and no forced publicity for winners ([SAFLII, CPA](https://www.saflii.org/za/legis/consol_act/cpa2008246/); [CDH](https://www.cliffedekkerhofmeyr.com/news/publications/2020/corporate/corporate-and-commercial-alert-22-january-Rules-of-the-game-Keep-the-consumer-protection-act-in-mind-when-facilitating-promotional-competitions.html)).

**Four questions remain open, and each is worth a single call.** Can a non-member use the ARB's pre-clearance service? Do Instagram stories count as the "small screen format" that allows the token on its own? What do Meta's organic (non-ad) standards say about alcohol brand posts? And what co-promotion terms do Checkers and Sixty60 impose? The researchers could not search the ARB rulings database directly, so the South African precedents above come from press reports and may not be complete.

## Conclusion

The "fix" story was a symptom. The underlying problem was the lack of a shared rule and a second pair of eyes, and both are now cheap to put in place. A single structural test, that the can never changes anything, holds in every jurisdiction examined, and it doubles as the brand's best creative brief: the evidence shows that the social register is where alcohol content performs, and the code's own permissions (the braai, everyday occasions, a "well-lived life") describe Doña Fuego's world almost word for word. The larger exposures were always in the brand's own plan rather than its slang: the beach, the 7% headline on three draft ad sets, and the pool and underwater frames the shoot found most distinctive.

The Non-Alc Margarita, which brands usually treat as their responsible get-out, works the other way in South Africa: it pulls a non-alcoholic product under the alcohol code and takes the driver line away. For a small non-member brand on a single retailer's shelf, the realistic enforcement is a complaint, discomfort at the retailer and a screenshot in the ad-ban debate rather than a formal sanction. Adopting the stricter 2026 text now costs little and gives the "No BS" promise the credibility it claims.
